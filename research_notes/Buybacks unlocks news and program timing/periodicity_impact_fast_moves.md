## 1. Heston, Korajczyk & Sadka (2010): the half-hour return periodicity in detail

### Takeaway
A stock that beat (lagged) other stocks in a given half-hour tends to beat (lag) them again in the same half-hour on each of the next ~40 trading days, by about 3 bps per half-hour on day 1 for the top-minus-bottom decile and about 1 bp on days 2-5; it is a cross-sectional, relative-return effect that is several times larger in the first and last half-hours and small (but significant) midday. It says nothing about an opening drive persisting later the same day: the same-day relation to the preceding half-hours is negative (reversal).

### Cited Findings
- FACT. Sample: all NYSE-listed common stocks (CRSP share codes 10/11) matched to TAQ, January 2001 to December 2005, 1,715 firms; 13 half-hour intervals 9:30-16:00; overnight returns excluded. — [HKS, arXiv 1005.3535](https://arxiv.org/abs/1005.3535)
- FACT. Method is cross-sectional (Jegadeesh-style) regression of half-hour return on the stock's own half-hour return k intervals ago, averaged Fama-MacBeth style. The authors state: "using cross-sectional regression in this way is different from measuring the autocorrelation of stock returns. In particular, the cross-sectional regression subtracts an overall market effect ... and focuses on returns relative to other stocks." — [HKS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1005.3535)
- FACT. Return responses are negative at the first several lags, then positive with peaks at exact multiples of 13 half-hours (lags 13, 26, 39, 52, 65). Over the first week the smallest Fama-MacBeth t-statistic at the daily lags is 9.62. "Temporary price pressure is reversed at virtually all future times except at the same time interval on subsequent days." — [HKS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1005.3535)
- FACT. Size: winners-minus-losers decile spread (sorted on one half-hour return) earns 3.01 bps in the same half-hour the next day. Day-1 decile detail: prior-day same-interval losers lose a further 1.35 bps, winners earn 1.66 bps. For lags of 2-5 days the spread stays above 1 bp per half-hour with t-statistics above 8.7. Decile-sort results are shown out to about 520 lags (roughly two trading months); the abstract states the effect lasts 40 trading days. — [HKS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1005.3535)
- FACT. Time of day: the day-1 daily spread earns over 11 bps in the opening half-hour and over 8 bps in the closing half-hour; from 10:00 to 15:30 it is positive in every half-hour but averages only 1.75 bps. The authors: the periodicity "is stronger at the beginning and end of the trading day ... it exists at all times." For midday intervals the coefficients are already positive at lags 10-13 (anticipation). After market-risk adjustment the day-5 spread is 4.84 bps in the first half-hour, 3.42 bps in the last, and under 1 bp midday (still significant). — [HKS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1005.3535)
- FACT. Short-lag reversal: the "nondaily" strategy sorted on the average return over lags 1-12 (the prior 24 hours excluding the same interval) loses: worst losers earn +3.16 bps, best winners lose 1.51 bps per half-hour; risk-adjusted decile spread is -4.65 bps. By time of day the day-1 nondaily strategy loses over 8 bps in the opening half-hour, 11 bps near the close, and 3.74 bps per half-hour midday. — [HKS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1005.3535)
- FACT. How much of the short-lag reversal is real: using bid-to-bid, ask-to-ask and midquote returns, "the only reversal that is statistically significant is that for k = 1," which the authors read as the market taking "longer than 30 minutes, but less than 60 minutes, to restore shocks to liquidity." All reversals beyond lag 1 disappear with quote-based returns and are attributed to bid-ask bounce; once bounce is removed there is "some evidence of momentum" at those lags. — [HKS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1005.3535)
- FACT. Volume, order imbalance, volatility: volume and volatility show the same daily periodicity; the level of order imbalance does not show obvious periodicity, but changes in order imbalance do. Adding these as regressors leaves the return pattern essentially unaffected ("do not explain the return patterns"). — [HKS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1005.3535)
- FACT. Robustness: present in small, mid and large caps; stronger in non-S&P 500 stocks (day-1 daily spread 3.28 bps vs 2.19 bps for S&P 500 names); for S&P 500 stocks the closing half-hour dominates (last-period to intraday ratio 9.61, i.e. 9.42 vs 0.98 bps). Present on every weekday (2.62 bps Tuesday to 3.44 bps Wednesday) and every calendar month (1.70 bps March to 7.80 bps November). Excluding low-priced stocks cuts day-1 to 1.93 bps (t = 10.34). Statistical significance is greatest post-decimalization; the 1993-1997 (one-eighth tick) period has the smallest coefficients and t-statistics. — [HKS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1005.3535)
- FACT. Not a net-of-cost trading strategy: after paying spreads the day-1 daily and nondaily decile spreads return about -24 to -25 bps; the authors frame the result as an execution-timing saving "equivalent of the effective spread," comparable to the 1.7 bp typical quoted half-spread of S&P 500 stocks (May 2008) and a 1 cent commission on a $30 stock. — [HKS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1005.3535)
- INTERPRETATION (authors). Autocorrelated institutional fund flows plus repeated use of the same execution schedule/algorithm at the same time each day produce repeating price pressure. Supporting facts they cite: 92.5% of a sample of institutional orders complete the same day they start (Breen, Hodrick, Korajczyk 2002); algorithmic trading was about one-third of US equity volume in 2007; crossing networks cross at set times; index funds trade at the close. The authors say none of their candidate explanations "fully explain the periodicity." — [HKS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1005.3535)
- Follow-up, theory (abstract only). Bogousslavsky (2016, Journal of Finance 71(6)) builds a model where a fraction of agents rebalance only once a day; it reproduces the HKS same-time-of-day periodicity. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:71:y:2016:i:6:p:2967-3006)
- Follow-up, mechanism evidence (abstract only). Murphy & Thirumalai (2017, Journal of Financial Research 40(4)) use data with masked trader IDs and trader type: net orders from repetitive institutional traders predict returns in the same half-hour on later days and absorb the predictability; attributed to parent orders executed over multiple days. Sample market and period not confirmed. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfnres:v:40:y:2017:i:4:p:455-477)
- Related but different result (abstract only). Gao, Han, Li, Zhou (2018, JFE 129(2)) "Market intraday momentum": for the S&P 500 ETF, 1993-2013, the first half-hour return measured from the prior close predicts the last half-hour return the same day. This is a market-level time-series result. Reported predictive R-squared is about 1.6% in a working-paper version (2.6% adding the twelfth half-hour); out-of-sample figures differ by version (1.2% vs 1.8% quoted in secondary summaries). — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:129:y:2018:i:2:p:394-414); [Alpha Architect summary](https://alphaarchitect.com/2014/08/attention-prop-traders-the-first-half-hour-of-trading-predicts-the-last-half-hour/)
- Related (abstract only). Bogousslavsky (2021, JFE 141(1)) uses about thirty years of US intraday data and finds size and illiquidity premia are realized in the last 30 minutes, tied to institutional constraints and overnight risk. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/article/eeejfinec/v_3a141_3ay_3a2021_3ai_3a1_3ap_3a172-194.htm)
- Related (abstract only). Baltussen, Da, Soebhag "End-of-Day Reversal" (2024 conference paper) reports strong intraday reversal in individual stocks at the end of the trading day. — [EFMA paper](http://www.efmaefm.org/0EFMAMEETINGS/EFMA%20ANNUAL%20MEETINGS/2024-Lisbon/papers/EndofDayReversal_withnames.pdf)

### Inferences
- The HKS effect does not say an opening drive continues through the day. It says the same clock window tends to repeat its relative (cross-sectional) sign on following days. Within the day the documented relation to the immediately preceding half-hour is a true reversal (quote-based, lag 1 only), and beyond that roughly nothing once bid-ask bounce is removed.
- Magnitudes are tiny relative to a discretionary intraday trade: 3 bps average, about 11 bps at the open and 8 bps at the close, for a decile long-short portfolio of roughly 170 stocks a side. For one stock on one day the effect is statistically invisible. Its practical meaning for a tape reader is "a real program that bought the first half-hour yesterday is somewhat more likely to be buying the first half-hour today," not a price forecast.
- Because it is measured relative to other stocks, market-wide moves are subtracted out. It is not evidence that the index repeats its half-hour pattern.
- The same-day market-level effect (Gao et al.) is a separate, weak result (R-squared of 1-3%) on SPY, not on single stocks.

### Gaps
- Table I regression coefficients by lag (exact bps at lags 1, 2, 13, 26...) were in figures/tables not captured by text extraction; only the decile-spread numbers above are verified.
- No out-of-sample replication of HKS on US data through the 2010s-2020s was found or opened. Bogousslavsky (2016) empirical sample and coefficients could not be read (host blocked). Khademalomoom & Narayan: no paper located in searches; treat as unverified.
- Whether the effect has shrunk since 2005 with closing-auction growth and modern algos: no source found.

## 2. Clock-time effects: do reversals or volatility cluster at round times?

### Takeaway
Documented: volume and trade counts spike at round clock marks, and volume/volatility are U-shaped with the open and close dominating. Not documented in anything found here: that price reversals cluster at 10:00, 10:30, 11:00, 15:00 or 15:30. That part is trader folklore plus plausible mechanism (scheduled data, order start/stop times), not a measured result.

### Cited Findings
- FACT (abstract and digest only). Broussard & Nikiforov (2014), "Intraday periodicity in algorithmic trading," Journal of International Financial Markets, Institutions and Money 30, 196-204: Nanex data on 30 liquid US stocks and 3 ETFs, 2003-2006; volume and number of trades spike by about 20% at regular 5 or 10 minute intervals, at round marks (hourly, half-hourly, 15, 10, 5 minutes). Transaction-cost measures show no significant change during the spikes. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:intfin:v:30:y:2014:i:c:p:196-204); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/intfin/v30y2014icp196-204.html)
- INTERPRETATION (those authors). Algorithms set by humans with a bias to round time marks; a CFA Institute digest called that explanation speculative. — [CFA digest](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/cfa-digest/2014/07/intraday-periodicity-in-algorithmic-trading-digest-summary)
- FACT. HKS find volume and volatility have daily periodicity at the half-hour grid, and note that crossing networks "cross at certain times of the day" and that index funds trade at the close. — [HKS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1005.3535)
- FACT. Reporting artifact showing start/stop conventions: in the ANcerno institutional database a large block of metaorders were all stamped 9:30 AM to 4:20 PM (exactly 410 minutes); the authors judged these times unreliable placeholders and dropped them. In the cleaned sample there is "a small bump" in the duration distribution for all-day metaorders. — [Zarinelli et al., arXiv 1412.2152](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.2152)
- FACT. Extreme intraday moves found with a plain percentage filter cluster in "the first or last couple of minutes of the trading day because of the U-shape intraday volatility distribution"; a volatility-relative filter alone clusters them around noon. — [Zawadowski et al., arXiv cond-mat/0406696](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0406696)
- FACT. NYSE closing auction: the Closing Auction Imbalance Freeze Time is 10 minutes before the close (3:50 p.m. ET), which is the MOC/LOC entry cutoff and the time the MOC/LOC imbalance is published; informational imbalance updates then publish every second if changed. (An NYSE timeline page also lists imbalance publication beginning at 3:00 p.m.; the search excerpts conflict on which feed starts when.) — [NYSE closing process fact sheet](https://www.nyse.com/publicdocs/nyse/NYSE_Auctions_Closing_Process_Fact_Sheet.pdf); [NYSE auctions page](https://www.nyse.com/trade/auctions)
- FACT. Interval VWAP in TCA: "An all-day VWAP would measure the average price from the open to the close ... Traders more often use interval VWAP, which looks at the specific trading interval, from the time they enter the market to the time they exit." — [Northern Trust](https://www.northerntrust.com/united-states/insights-research/2026/asset-servicing/its/trade-execution-benchmarks)
- FACT. Talos defines Market VWAP as "the interval VWAP ... across the client parent order time duration"; with a limit price only in-limit trades count. KX: VWAP can be computed over full session, order life, a specified intraday interval, or an opening/closing window, so the period must be stated. — [Talos](https://www.talos.com/insights/execution-insights-through-transaction-cost-analysis-tca-benchmarks-and-slippage); [KX](https://kx.com/?p=17929)
- FACT. Bloomberg: interval VWAP "does not account for order timing"; switching the benchmark from VWAP to arrival price flips an order from outperforming to underperforming (or vice versa) 41% of the time. — [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/professional/?p=47069)

### Inferences
- The only hard evidence is about activity (volume, trade count) at round marks, from a 2003-2006 sample. Activity spikes with no change in transaction costs do not imply price turning points.
- A reasonable mechanism for turns near round times is the end of the price concession when a scheduled order stops (see section 3) plus scheduled information. This is a hypothesis; no study found tests "reversal frequency by clock minute."
- The interval VWAP that a desk's algo is judged against is anchored at that order's start time, so it generally differs from the session VWAP on a chart. Reading the session VWAP as "the level institutions are benchmarked to" is an approximation at best.

### Gaps
- Scheduled release times were not verified from calendars in this session (hosts blocked or not returned by search). From background knowledge only, unverified here: ISM PMIs, consumer sentiment, JOLTS, new home sales at 10:00 ET; EIA weekly petroleum status report at 10:30 ET Wednesdays; most Treasury coupon auctions close at 13:00 ET; FOMC statement at 14:00 ET with press conference at 14:30 ET; European cash equity close at 11:30 ET. The report writer should cite the agencies' calendars before publishing these.
- No academic evidence found that reversals cluster at 10:00, 10:30, 11:00, 15:00 or 15:30 ET, or around the European close, in US single stocks.
- No data found on the share of institutional parent orders that start or end on the hour or half-hour.
- No post-2006 replication of the round-time volume spike was found.

## 3. Why price impact partly reverts after a large order ends

### Takeaway
While a large order is working, liquidity providers only absorb it at a worsening price; when the flow stops the concession starts decaying immediately. On average about one-third of the peak impact is gone by the same day's close, and after removing follow-on same-direction orders roughly two-thirds is gone within several weeks; orders known to carry no information revert almost fully within 2-5 days. The decay is a slow power law in volume time, not a snap-back.

### Cited Findings
- FACT (model). Farmer, Gerig, Lillo & Waelbroeck: "fair pricing" condition says "the average transaction price of the metaorder is equal to the price after trading is completed." With a Pareto order-size tail exponent of 1.5 the model gives square-root impact and "the permanent impact is equal to two thirds of the maximum immediate impact, i.e., following the completion of a metaorder the price should revert by one third from its peak value." Fair pricing means market makers break even on metaorders of every size. — [FGLW, arXiv 1102.5457](https://arxiv.org/abs/1102.5457)
- FACT (competing models, as summarized by Bucci et al.). In the latent order book and propagator models the "reaction" impact decays as a power law of time to a small asymptotic value; the long-run level depends on the information in the trade, hence the split into mechanical "reaction impact" and "prediction impact." — [Bucci et al., arXiv 1901.05332](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05332)
- FACT. Bucci, Benzaquen, Lillo, Bouchaud (2019): ANcerno data, about 8 million single-day metaorders, Russell 3000 stocks, January 2007 to June 2010 (880 trading days), about 5% of total market volume. Ratio of start-to-close impact to start-to-end (peak) impact averages 0.66 +/- 0.04. "Relaxation takes place as soon as the metaorder ends." — [Bucci et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05332)
- FACT. Intraday decay speed in that paper: decay is continuous in z = (market volume traded after the order ends) / (market volume traded during the order) and is fitted by (1+z)^(1-beta) - z^(1-beta) with beta = 0.22. Restricting to z between 0 and 2 the decay "appears to saturate around the 2/3 value," but over wider windows impact keeps falling. The ratio rises with order size because bigger orders finish later and have less time to decay before the close. — [Bucci et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05332)
- FACT. Multi-day: overnight contributes little (decay runs in volume time); impact at next-day close is about 4/5 of impact at the execution-day close; after deconvolving the multi-day same-sign autocorrelation of metaorders, the fit converges at about 50 days to roughly 1/2 of the end-of-day-1 impact, i.e. about 1/3 of peak. By size bucket the asymptote relative to day-1 close is about 0.44 large caps, 0.61 mid caps, 0.35 small caps. Error bars are large at 50 days (one fit gives an asymptote of 0.0 +/- 0.19). The authors find "no support" for a 2/3 permanent impact. — [Bucci et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05332)
- FACT (cited within Bucci et al.). Brokmann et al., on one manager's (CFM) metaorders, after removing the manager's alpha and correlated trades, found impact decayed to about 0.15 of its initial value after 15 days. — [Bucci et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05332)
- FACT. Zarinelli, Treccani, Farmer, Lillo (2015): ANcerno, January 2007 to December 2009, 6,944,883 filtered metaorders, all completed within one day. Followed for up to three times the order's duration (same day only), the price decays to a level "remarkably close to (though slightly higher than) 2/3 of the peak impact." Long, low-participation orders relax more slowly and stay higher; for the highest participation rates impact "relaxes toward zero" with no flattening in the window, matching the propagator model. Sometimes the price starts reverting before the order ends, which the authors attribute to front-loaded (non-VWAP) execution schedules. — [Zarinelli et al., arXiv 1412.2152](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.2152)
- INTERPRETATION (Zarinelli et al. footnote). Low-participation orders revert more slowly because their end is harder for others to detect. — [Zarinelli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.2152)
- FACT. Moro et al. (2009), London Stock Exchange and Spanish BME (not US): impact grows as a power law during execution and "after the order is finished, it reverts to a level of about 0.5-0.7 of its value at its peak." — [Moro et al., arXiv 0908.0202](https://arxiv.org/abs/0908.0202)
- FACT (abstract only). Gomes & Waelbroeck (2015, Quantitative Finance 15(5)): institutional trades with about a quarter labelled cash-flow trades. During execution the shape and scale of impact are similar for cash-flow and other metaorders; afterwards they differ: for cash flows "the impact reverts almost completely on average in two to five days." — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/quantf/v15y2015i5p773-793.html)
- FACT (abstract only). Bershova & Rakhlin (2013, Quantitative Finance 13(11)): large US institutional orders from buy-side flow; impact rises roughly as the square root of trade duration; results support the FGLW theory with permanent impact about 2/3 of peak. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:quantf:v:13:y:2013:i:11:p:1759-1778)
- INTERPRETATION (Bucci et al. conclusion). For a truly informed order there should be no decay (the price was pushed to its correct level); for an uninformed order impact should decay back to zero; the observed average plateau is a blend weighted by the fraction of informed orders. If impact is fully transient the average cost of the trade is 2/3 of peak impact. — [Bucci et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05332)
- FACT. HKS independent estimate of resiliency for ordinary liquidity shocks in NYSE stocks 2001-2005: quote-based reversal is significant only at the first half-hour lag, "longer than 30 minutes, but less than 60 minutes, to restore shocks to liquidity." — [HKS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1005.3535)

### Inferences
- Researcher's arithmetic from the Bucci fitted curve (beta = 0.22), fraction of peak impact remaining versus market volume traded after the order ends, expressed as a multiple z of the volume traded while it ran: z = 0.1 about 0.91; z = 0.25 about 0.85; z = 0.5 about 0.79; z = 1 about 0.72; z = 2 about 0.64; z = 5 about 0.54; z = 10 about 0.47. So for an order that ran 30 minutes, expect on average only about 10% of the peak impact back within the next few minutes, 20-30% after another 15-30 minutes of comparable volume, and roughly one-third by the time twice the order's volume has traded. These are averages over millions of orders with enormous dispersion.
- Plain language: the first part of the bounce is the fastest in percentage-per-minute terms but it is not large. The documented average same-day give-back is about one-third of the push, not a return to the starting price.
- Reversion size is conditional on why the order existed. Liquidity/cash-flow selling tends to fully retrace over days; informed or news-backed selling is expected to retrace little. The tape alone does not reveal which it is; impact during execution looks the same for both (Gomes & Waelbroeck).
- All of these datasets are institutional orders mostly below 30% participation executed by scheduling algos. They are not samples of panic liquidations or stop cascades, so applying the 1/3 figure to a waterfall is extrapolation.

### Gaps
- No direct minute-by-minute (clock time) decay table was found; the only quantitative intraday curve is in volume time.
- Gomes & Waelbroeck and Bershova & Rakhlin full texts not opened; sample periods and exact reversion numbers unverified beyond abstracts.
- "Trades, Quotes and Prices" (Bouchaud, Bonart, Donier, Gould) not opened; propagator and latent-order-book descriptions here come from the papers above.
- No post-2010 US metaorder decay study was found.

## 4. Typical metaorder duration and size statistics

### Takeaway
Institutional parent orders are overwhelmingly single-day and typically short: median execution time about 35 minutes in the ANcerno sample, with heavy-tailed duration and participation distributions, and the dataset itself is only about 5% of market volume.

### Cited Findings
- FACT. Median execution time of ANcerno metaorders is 35 minutes; median duration in volume time is about 0.09 of the day's volume clock. — [Bucci et al., "Crossover from linear to square-root market impact," arXiv 1811.05230](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.05230)
- FACT. Zarinelli et al. sample: 28,386,564 metaorders before filtering, 6,944,883 after (Russell 3000, ending before 4:01 PM, lasting more than 2 minutes, participation rate below 0.3), January 2007 to December 2009; all completed within one trading day. For the top 20 stocks ANcerno metaorders are about 5% of daily volume. — [Zarinelli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.2152)
- FACT. Participation rate and duration are each approximately truncated power-law distributed (fitted exponents -0.864 for participation over 0.0001 to 0.1, and -0.932 for duration over 0.01 to 0.5 of the day): "substantial variability" over orders of magnitude. The daily fraction (order size / day volume) is less fat-tailed and "clearly not a power law." Correlation between participation rate and duration is -0.022: "very aggressive metaorders are typically short and long metaorders more often have a small participation rate." — [Zarinelli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.2152)
- FACT. On a sample stock (AAPL, March-April 2008) there is "almost always an active metaorder" from the database at any minute, and overlapping metaorders tend to share the same sign (herding). — [Zarinelli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.2152)
- FACT. 92.5% of a sample of institutional orders are completed the same day trading starts (Breen, Hodrick, Korajczyk 2002, as cited by HKS). — [HKS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1005.3535)
- FACT. Metaorder signs are autocorrelated over many days (fitted as an exponentially truncated power law), i.e. the same side tends to come back on following days. — [Bucci et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05332)
- FACT. Square-root law used across these papers: impact = Y x daily volatility x (Q / daily volume)^delta with Y "of order unity" and delta 0.4 to 0.7, typically about 0.5; total size Q matters far more than duration or participation rate separately. — [Zarinelli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.2152)
- FACT. Zarinelli et al. conclude the typical broker schedule in the data is front-loaded, not a flat VWAP profile. — [Zarinelli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.2152)

### Inferences
- A single-day ANcerno "metaorder" is one broker ticket for one day; the true parent decision often spans several days (sign autocorrelation, Murphy & Thirumalai), which is the link between sections 1 and 4.
- With a 35-minute median and a 2-minute floor, many tickets are far shorter than a "VWAP all day" order; all-day orders exist but are a visible minority bump.

### Gaps
- Median participation rate and median share of daily volume were not found as numbers in the text read (they are in figures). What predicts duration (size, urgency, volatility) is not quantified in the sources opened beyond the near-zero participation/duration correlation.
- These statistics are 2007-2010; no current (2020s) public equivalent found.

## 5. Fast extreme moves: causes, reversal size and timing, and the VWAP-order arithmetic

### Takeaway
The best direct evidence (liquid NYSE/NASDAQ stocks, 2000-2002) is that after a drop of 7-11% inside an hour the average rebound was about 0.8-1.1% in 10 minutes and 1.1-2.1% within 60 minutes, i.e. roughly 15-20% of the drop, with nothing further on average from 60 to 120 minutes; rebound size scales with drop size. At daily horizons, moves without news tend to reverse and moves with news tend to drift. A 1 ATR move in 20-30 minutes is far too large to be the footprint of one scheduled VWAP/TWAP order under the square-root law.

### Cited Findings
- FACT. Zawadowski, Andor, Kertesz (Quantitative Finance 2006): NYSE TAQ 2000-2002 and NASDAQ, liquid stocks only (traded in at least 90% of minutes); event = price move over at most 60 minutes exceeding both 4% and 8 times that stock's normal 60-minute volatility for that time of day; first event per stock per day; first 5 and last 60 minutes of the session excluded. — [arXiv cond-mat/0406696](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0406696)
- FACT. NYSE (222 decreases, 175 increases): average event-hour abnormal return -7.2% for drops; rebound +0.84% in minutes 0-10, +1.13% in 0-30, +1.13% in 0-60, and +0.01% (not significant) in minutes 60-120. For rises (+3.1%): -0.73% in 0-10, -0.73% in 0-30, -0.63% in 0-60, -0.12% (not significant) in 60-120. — [Zawadowski et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0406696)
- FACT. NASDAQ (215 decreases, 273 increases): average drop -11.0%; rebound +1.06% in 0-10, +1.83% in 0-30, +2.15% in 0-60, +0.17% (not significant) in 60-120. For rises (+5.3%): -1.24% in 0-10, -1.51% in 0-30, -1.68% in 0-60. — [Zawadowski et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0406696)
- FACT. "The size of the reversal is positively dependent on the initial price change and compatible with a linear assumption." Volatility, volume and (NYSE) bid-ask spread jump at the event and stay elevated long after, with volatility decaying as a power law. — [Zawadowski et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0406696)
- FACT. Tradability: on the NYSE the spread widened enormously at the event and removed most contrarian profit; on NASDAQ the spread stayed roughly constant and buying at the ask and selling at the bid 60 minutes later earned 2.686% (t = 4.02; 159 events, 120-minute drops); entering two minutes late still earned 2.149% (t = 4.17). Depth at the quote was not measured. — [Zawadowski et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0406696)
- INTERPRETATION (those authors). "We can only speculate about the origin": behavioral trading during fast moves or interaction of informed and uninformed traders. They did not separate news from no-news events. — [Zawadowski et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0406696)
- FACT (abstract only). Brogaard, Carrion, Moyaert, Riordan, Shkilko, Sokolov (JFE 2018): Nasdaq HFT data 2008-2009, about 45,000 extreme price movements at 10-second intervals in 40 large stocks (99.9th percentile method). On average HFTs supply liquidity during EPMs, absorbing imbalances created by non-HFTs; this holds for single-stock EPMs, but when several stocks have simultaneous EPMs HFT liquidity demand dominates supply. Little evidence HFTs cause EPMs. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:128:y:2018:i:2:p:253-265)
- FACT. Mini flash crashes (Golub, Keane, Poon): Nanex definition is at least 10 consecutive down (up) ticks within 1.5 seconds and a move over 0.8%. In four volatile months (Sep, Oct, Nov 2008 and May 2010) there were 5,140 (2,760 up, 2,380 down), average move 2.45%; 67.85% classified as initiated by Intermarket Sweep Orders, about 87% of trades in them ISO-marked. Example: Goldman Sachs, 7 October 2008, 1.6% drop ($1.89) in under 400 milliseconds on 86,000 shares. — [arXiv 1211.6667](https://arxiv.org/abs/1211.6667)
- INTERPRETATION (Golub et al.). Caused by aggressive sweep orders hitting thin books under Reg NMS top-of-book-only protection, with fleeting liquidity. — [arXiv 1211.6667](https://arxiv.org/abs/1211.6667)
- FACT (abstract only). Grant, Wolf, Yu (Journal of Banking & Finance 2005): US stock index futures, November 1987 to September 2002; highly significant intraday reversals after large price changes at the open, stronger after large positive opens; significance "drops sharply" after a bid-ask cost adjustment. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jbfina:v:29:y:2005:i:5:p:1311-1327)
- FACT (abstract and secondary only; daily/monthly horizons, not intraday). Chan (2003, JFE 70): stocks with large moves and no identifiable public news show subsequent reversal; stocks with news show drift, strongest after bad news and in smaller, less liquid stocks. — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v70y2003i2p223-260.html)
- FACT (abstract and digest only). Savor (2012, JFE 106(3)): major price shock = daily abnormal return beyond +/-10%; classified by whether analyst reports accompany it. "Price events accompanied by information are followed by drift, while no-information ones result in reversals"; drift only when the price move and the analyst revision share a sign; combined strategy about 35% annualized abnormal return. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:106:y:2012:i:3:p:635-659); [CFA digest](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/cfa-digest/2013/02/stock-returns-after-major-price-shocks-the-impact-of-information-digest-summary)
- FACT (abstract only). Tetlock (2010, RFS 23(9)): using Dow Jones newswire days, return reversal is reduced on news days, more so for small firms and for stories with many newswire messages. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:23:y:2010:i:9:p:3520-3557)
- FACT (abstract only). Nagel (2012, RFS 25(7)) "Evaporating Liquidity": short-term reversal strategy returns proxy for the return to liquidity provision; the expected return is strongly time-varying and "highly predictable with the VIX index," rising sharply in turmoil such as 2007-09. A separate conference paper confirming Nagel reports one VIX point higher implies 5.37 bps higher reversal return over the next five days. — [NBER w17653](https://www.nber.org/papers/W17653); [NBER conference paper](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f103855/f103855.pdf)
- FACT. Square-root law and typical constants as in section 4 (Y of order one). — [Zarinelli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.2152)

### Inferences
- VWAP-order arithmetic (researcher's calculation, using impact about 1 x daily sigma x sqrt(Q / daily volume)). A full-day VWAP order for 10% of daily volume has expected total impact of about sqrt(0.10) = 0.32 daily sigma spread over 6.5 hours. In any 20-minute midday window it trades only about 0.3-0.5% of the day's volume, an incremental push on the order of 0.05-0.07 sigma. To produce about 1 daily sigma of impact the square-root law needs Q near a full day's volume, which cannot trade in 20 minutes at scheduled participation rates (the datasets cap at 30%). ATR is larger than close-to-close sigma, which makes the gap wider. Conclusion: a 1+ ATR move away from VWAP inside 20-30 minutes is not what one passive schedule looks like. It requires some mix of news, urgent/implementation-shortfall style selling at very high participation, many same-direction orders at once (herding is documented), and liquidity providers pulling back so each share moves price more.
- Expectation setting for "turn" trades after a waterfall, from the only direct study: the average bounce was about one-seventh to one-fifth of the drop, most of it in the first 10-30 minutes, and essentially zero additional drift after an hour. A typical retracement to VWAP or of half the move is not the documented average. The sample (2000-2002, moves of 4%+ and 8 sigma) is old and more extreme than a 1 ATR flush.
- The stopping of the push matters more than the clock: section 3 says decay starts when the flow ends. A bounce attempt while the seller is still active is fighting continuing impact; the studies time minute 0 at the end of the move, which a live trader cannot know in advance. Reported averages therefore overstate what is capturable in real time.
- Conditional on news, the daily-horizon literature points to continuation, and Gomes & Waelbroeck point to little reversion for informed flow; conditional on no news or liquidity-driven flow, to reversal. The intraday studies found did not make that split, so "no-news flushes bounce more intraday" is a consistent extrapolation, not a measured intraday fact.
- Nagel implies bounce (liquidity-provision) returns are larger when VIX is high; Brogaard et al. imply liquidity support is weakest when many stocks are moving together. Both push the same way: bigger potential reversal, less reliable support, in market-wide stress.
- Stop-loss cascades, short squeezes and forced liquidation, ETF/index arbitrage and option-hedging flows are commonly given causes of fast moves. No source opened here quantifies their contribution to single-stock intraday flushes; treat as plausible mechanisms, not documented magnitudes.

### Gaps
- No modern (post-2010) US single-stock study of rebound fraction and time-to-rebound after 20-30 minute moves, and none scaled by ATR, was found.
- Brogaard et al.: the share of EPMs that reverse versus persist was not retrievable (abstract only); one secondary summary describes the threshold as the top 1% rather than 99.9th percentile, which conflicts.
- Chan, Tetlock, Savor magnitudes (percent reversal/drift by horizon) not verified; a figure of roughly 80 bps reversal after extreme no-news moves appeared in a secondary excerpt without a traceable source and should not be used.
- Grant-Wolf-Yu magnitudes not available. Golub et al. do not report rebound statistics in the text scanned.
- No quantified evidence found on stop cascades or dealer gamma hedging as causes of intraday single-stock waterfalls.

## 6. Capitulation or climax volume as a turning-point marker

### Takeaway
No rigorous intraday study was found showing that a volume climax marks the turn. What is documented is weaker: volume and volatility peak at the extreme move and stay elevated afterwards, bigger moves get bigger bounces, and at the daily level it is extreme order imbalance, not volume itself, that precedes reversal.

### Cited Findings
- FACT. Around intraday extreme events volume, volatility and (NYSE) spread "increase sharply at the event" and "stay significantly high long after the price adjustment has taken place"; volatility decays as a power law (Omori-like), volume does not follow a clean power law. — [Zawadowski et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0406696)
- FACT. Reversal size is roughly linear in the size of the initial move. — [Zawadowski et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0406696)
- FACT (secondary excerpt of Chordia & Subrahmanyam, daily data). Market returns "reverse themselves after high-negative-imbalance, large-negative-return days," and "the relation between returns and volume runs through order imbalances rather than through sheer trading volume." — [Chordia et al. PDF](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~mkearns/finread/Chordia_buy-sell_orders.pdf)
- FACT (abstract only; non-US). On the Shenzhen exchange (2003) volume, spread and volume imbalance peak around extreme intraday price changes and relax slowly as a power law, with significant reversal and a permanent component. — [arXiv 1003.0168](https://arxiv.org/abs/1003.0168)
- FACT. Zarinelli et al. cite ANcerno-based evidence that intense institutional sell herds are followed by return reversals while returns from intense buy herds are permanent (weekly horizon). — [Zarinelli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.2152)

### Inferences
- High volume at the low is close to a tautology in these samples (events are defined by the move, and volume peaks with it), and volume remains high afterwards, so "volume has peaked" is hard to identify in real time and is not shown to add predictive power beyond the size of the move.
- The evidence favors signed flow (imbalance exhaustion, i.e. the seller finishing) over raw volume as the relevant variable, which matches the impact-decay mechanism in section 3. "Selling climax" as a chart pattern remains practitioner lore without a located controlled test.

### Gaps
- No US intraday study testing volume-climax or order-imbalance-exhaustion signals for turning points was found. Chordia & Subrahmanyam was seen only as a search excerpt.
