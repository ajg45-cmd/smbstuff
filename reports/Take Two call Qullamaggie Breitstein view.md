# Both traders would skip this entry

Judged by their published rules, neither Kristjan Kullamägi (Qullamaggie) nor Lance Breitstein would have bought the Take-Two June 17, 2027 $270 calls when and how they were bought, and both of their exit methods are already at or past their first trigger. That is an inference: **neither trader is known to have said anything about this trade**, and everything below about what they "would think" is reasoned from their rules. Qullamaggie buys strength after the market has reacted to a surprise, trades stock and says options "are not for me", and risks 0.25-1% of his account per trade; a far out-of-the-money call bought into a two-day, 10.5% slide the day before a scheduled event fits none of his three setups. Breitstein does use options and does buy sharp declines, but only after the turn is confirmed (his "right side of the V"), with size matched to a graded setup and an exit chosen in advance; applying his intraday methods to a seven-month option is extrapolation. On the levels found, Take-Two closed launch day at $208.65, below the prior day's low of $210.50, about $1 above its 50-day average and below both its approximate 10-day average and its 200-day average. A Breitstein-style prior-day-low trail therefore triggered on launch day, and a Qullamaggie-style moving-average trail has only the 50-day left beneath the price. My own view is that an option like this should be managed with daily closing prices on the stock, a partial sale into the first rally toward $222-231, and a deadline before the February earnings report, because time decay and falling implied volatility can sink the option while the stock chart still looks intact. This report explains two methods and a set of scenarios for education; it is not personalized investment advice.

## Qullamaggie buys surprises after the gap, and this was neither

### His rules reward strength, small risk and a moving-average trail

Qullamaggie's published method has three setups. The first is a breakout from a tight consolidation after a stock has already risen roughly 30-100% in one to three months. The second is the episodic pivot, which his blog introduces this way: "When unexpected good news hits a stock, especially if that stock is neglected, that can trigger multi-month, and multi-year moves." Its listed requirements are a gap up of 10% or more and big volume, and he prefers a stock that has not rallied in the prior three to six months. The third is the parabolic short, with a rarer long version after a stock has fallen 50-60% or more in a few days ([qullamaggie.com](https://qullamaggie.com/my-3-timeless-setups-that-have-made-me-tens-of-millions/); [mirror copy](https://qullamaggie.net/3-timeless-setups-that-have-made-me-tens-of-millions/)). Third-party guides say he screens for the top 1-2% of stocks by performance over one, three and six months ([Breakouts Happen](https://breakoutshappen.com/stock-news/how-to-trade-like-qullamaggie-setups-strategy-and-screener)).

His entry is the break of the opening range high, with the stop at the low of the day. Secondary sources add that he skips a trade whose stop would be wider than about one average daily range ([Snappchart](https://www.snappchart.app/blog/beginner-playbook/qullamaggie-trading-strategy)). Search summaries of his blog report that most positions are **10-20% of the account, that risk is usually 0.25-1% per trade**, and that he would not hold more than 30% in one name overnight ([qullamaggie.com](https://qullamaggie.com/my-3-timeless-setups-that-have-made-me-tens-of-millions/)).

His exit rule is the best-documented part: "You should sell 1/3 to 1/2 of the position after 3-5 days, and then move the stop to break even. The rest of the position should be trailed with the 10- or the 20-day moving average. Depends on how fast the stock is." ([quoted by AsymTrading on X](https://x.com/AsymTrading/status/1706716710735085833?lang=en)). The trail fires on the first daily close below the average, not on an intraday dip; interview notes have him checking 10 to 15 minutes before the close ([Trading Resource Hub transcript](https://tradingresourcehub.substack.com/p/qullamaggie-chat-with-traders-reimagined-transcript)). For episodic pivots his blog tells readers to build their own sell rules and mentions trailing with the 20- or 50-day average ([qullamaggie.com](https://qullamaggie.com/my-3-timeless-setups-that-have-made-me-tens-of-millions/)).

Three more stated views bear directly on this trade. In a stream transcript he says he trades stocks and sometimes leveraged funds, and that options "are not for me" ([stream transcripts on Scribd](https://www.scribd.com/document/748079660/All-Qullamaggie-streams-transcripts)). On earnings he posted: "I only hold through earnings if I have a good profit on the stock and have sold a partial for profit already." ([Qullamaggie on X](https://x.com/Qullamaggie/status/1587130234574356485)). And a compilation of his teaching videos says to avoid longs when the 10-day average slopes down or the stock has broken below its 20-day; this last source is an auto-generated video summary and the weakest of the group ([Swing School summary](https://videohighlight.com/v/WFfGHO25oYY?mediaType=youtube&language=en&summaryType=default&summaryId=b7joQocBsSRa8p-RVClQ&aiFormatted=false)).

### Measured against those rules, the entry fails on every count but one

What follows is inference from the rules above. On **setup fit**, the trade matches none of the three patterns. Take-Two was about 22% below its July high and down roughly 15% for the year before launch ([Fox Business](https://www.foxbusiness.com/markets/take-two-stock-jumps-over-6-rockstars-long-awaited-gta-vi-arrives-after-13-year-wait)), so it was nowhere near a top-performers screen and was not consolidating tightly after a big advance. A 10.5% two-day drop is far short of the collapse he associates with a long bounce.

On **timing relative to the catalyst**, the logic runs backward from his. His episodic pivot is defined by surprise and is bought after the gap confirms it. A launch date known for a year is the opposite of unexpected news, and a purchase the day before has no gap, no volume confirmation and no opening range high. The sessions that came closest to his pattern were November 10, when the stock jumped as much as 10.6% intraday after management said pre-orders were far above plan ([Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/2026/11/10/why-take-two-stock-s-got-game-today/)), and November 18, which opened about 4% above the prior close on 12.0 million shares. The November 18 gap was under his 10% threshold, and a buyer using his stop at that day's low of $210.50 would have been stopped out on launch day when the stock traded down to $208.20.

On **trend and moving averages**, the stock at purchase had just lost its short-term averages and sat below its 200-day. On the **right side versus the left side of the decline**, he does not use that phrase, but his entry requires the stock to be rising through the day's opening high, so buying a slide before any upturn is the opposite sequence. On **vehicle**, he does not trade options at all, and nothing was found from him on strikes, expiries or time decay. His leverage comes from margin on stock with a tight stop, which has no expiry date and no strike to clear.

On **sizing**, his framework gives a clean test. Because the call was bought without a stock-price stop, the whole premium is the amount at risk, so the premium should be compared with his 0.25-1% of account. A position of $1,250 per contract fits that band only if the account is at least $125,000 per contract held. The one count on which the trade is compatible with his thinking is payoff shape. He accepts a 25-30% win rate because a few winners return many times the risk ([Laws of Swing notes](https://www.scribd.com/document/905644241/Qullamaggie-s-Laws-of-Swing)), and a long call that loses small and can win large has that profile, provided the stake is small.

### His exit rules, translated, leave only the 50-day average as support

No source shows how he would manage an option, so this plan applies his stock rules to the underlying share price and treats the option as the thing being sold when a rule fires.

**His hard stop is the entry day's low.** If the purchase was on November 17, that low was not found; the $203.93 close is the nearest proxy and the stock has not traded below it since. If the purchase was during the November 18 session, the stop was $210.50 and it was hit on launch day. This is the main reason the entry date matters.

**Right now, November 20**, his trailing averages give a weak picture. The stock is below the approximate 10-day average of $215.6-217.1, so the 10-day trail he recommends to beginners gives no reason to hold; under a strict reading he would already be out, or more likely never in. The only average of his still under the price is the 50-day at $207.64, which his blog allows as a trail for slower, news-driven positions. A plan in his style would therefore check the stock in the last 15 minutes of each session and **sell on the first daily close below about $207.64**, with $203.93 as the level where he gets out no matter what. An intraday dip below the 50-day that recovers by the close does not count.

**His partial sale comes after three to five days**, which for this position means roughly November 20-25. The rule assumes the trade is showing a gain by then. If an official sales announcement or a market rebound lifts the stock back toward the 200-day average at $218-221 or the launch-day high of $225.68 in that window, his rule says sell a third to half of the contracts into that strength and move the stop on the rest to breakeven, which here means the stock price at entry, $204-208. If no such strength appears, there is no profit to bank and the stop alone governs.

**The 10-day trail becomes usable only after the stock closes back above it.** Once the stock is above a rising 10-day average, the remainder is held until the first close below it, or below the 20-day if the advance is slow and that figure has been obtained. One piece of arithmetic helps here: the closes of $224.64 to $231.31 from November 10-13 drop out of the 10-day window over the next several sessions, so that average will fall toward the low $210s if the stock stays where it is, making a reclaim easier.

**For the February and May earnings reports**, his rule is explicit: hold through only with a good profit and a partial already sold. The earlier research estimated the February report for about February 2-9, 2027 and the May report for about May 13-20, based on last year's dates of February 3 and May 21; neither date has been announced ([Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260106677069/en/); [Business Wire](https://secure.businesswire.com/news/home/20260423775049/en/Take-Two-Interactive-Software-Inc.-to-Report-Fourth-Quarter-and-Fiscal-Year-2026-Results-on-Thursday-May-21-2026)). That research modeled the stock price needed for the option to be worth its $12.50 cost at about $229-243 in mid-February. So unless the stock is well above roughly $240 going into the February report and some contracts have been sold, his rule says to be out before it. Only a reduced, already-profitable remainder would be carried into May.

The trade he would actually take, by inference, is still ahead. If an official sales figure or the February report produces a gap of 10% or more on heavy volume, which from $208.65 means an open near $229.50 or higher, that is his episodic pivot. He would buy stock through the opening range high with a stop at that day's low.

## Breitstein would ask where the turn was and what the exit is

### His rules are about expected value, confirmed turns and graded size

Breitstein frames the job as estimating the expected value of an opportunity and betting in proportion to it. He rejects fixed win-rate or reward-to-risk targets, since the two trade off ([Trillium interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-jGUSk5lJbI)). The same logic governs exits. A snippet of one of his posts reads: "Your take-profit rules and stop should be dictated by your system based on maximizing EV. Not a tight stop, but the RIGHT stop," and calls "can't go broke taking profits" a fallacy ([X, March 2024](https://x.com/TheOneLanceB/status/1772277268909277312)).

His signature entry idea is the right side of the V. Buying a falling stock at "Point A", he writes, means "you are fighting the trend. Your probability of success is lower as a result. Your risk is potentially uncapped w no obvious stop"; the same price after the turn has the trend behind it and the low as a stop ([thread copy](https://en.rattibha.com/thread/1508815079541776397)). He lists ways to define the turn: "Breaking a trendline. Holding new side of VWAP. Break of prior bars. Break of a moving average. Capitulatory exhaustive price move OR exhaustive volume exchange." ([X, April 2022](https://x.com/TheOneLanceB/status/1516798713271767040)). He says the concept "applies to every single chart and every single decision as a trader. It applies to trailing stops." ([Thread Reader copy](https://threadreaderapp.com/thread/1546494443511062528.html)).

On size, he teaches steeply larger bets on rare top-grade setups and small or no bets on the rest, because "huge chunks of PNL come from an exceptionally small percent of the opps" ([X, January 2024](https://x.com/TheOneLanceB/status/1745466178934550746)). The cap is survival: a recent interview summary says he never sized a trade so big that a failure would ruin his psychology ([Churning and Burning](https://churningandburning.com/2026/11/churning-and-chatting-4-lance-breitstein-the-100-million-market-wizard.html)). He looks for edge in what he calls broken slot machines, pockets of the market that are in play and mispriced, usually on fresh news ([Trillium interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-jGUSk5lJbI)).

On exits, the best-supported rule is mechanical. Secondary sources describe him trailing a long below the prior bar's low on a two-minute chart, with a predetermined exit on more than nine trades in ten ([talk summary](https://videohighlight.com/v/R215f4fj7V8?mediaType=youtube&language=en&summaryType=default&aiFormatted=false); [trailingstoploss.com](https://trailingstoploss.com/traders/lance-breitstein/)). The research did **not** confirm any ladder of timeframes he moves through as a trade matures, any rule for scaling out in fractions, or any statement on holding through earnings or trading product launches.

Unlike Qullamaggie, he uses options heavily. He reported that options were 39% of his first-half 2024 profit and that "Selling options were a HUGE amount of my pnl. I'd guess about 20%!" ([X, June 2024](https://x.com/TheOneLanceB/status/1806759030452621590)). His podcast material stresses implied volatility, time decay, "and why direction alone doesn't guarantee an options profit" ([podcast page](https://creators.spotify.com/pod/profile/lance-breitstein/)). Nothing was found on him buying long-dated, far out-of-the-money calls as a position.

### The entry was early, in a crowded event, through expensive options

Again, this is inference. On **setup fit**, his long setups in falling stocks need capitulation: a fast, accelerating drop on heavy turnover over many consecutive legs ([Chat With Traders summary](https://sozai.app/transcript/ultimate-day-trader-lance-breitstein/)). Two down days totalling 10.5% that left the stock 22% off its high is an ordinary pullback by that standard.

On the **right side versus the left side of the V**, the answer depends on the entry date. A purchase at the November 17 close was made while the stock was still making lower lows, with no turn signal on the daily chart. That is the left side by his definition. A purchase after hours on November 18 came after a strong up day, which is better, but it bought the after-hours fade of that day instead of a confirmed higher low. On **timing relative to the catalyst**, his edge lives in dislocations and breaking news. A launch that a prediction market gave about 97% odds of setting a record, per the earlier research ([Benzinga](https://www.benzinga.com/markets/prediction-markets/26/11/63248840/gta-6-preorders)), is an efficiently priced event, not a broken slot machine.

On **vehicle**, he would have no objection to options as such. His objection would be to price. Implied volatility on near-dated options stood near 74% a week before launch ([Market Chameleon](https://marketchameleon.com/Overview/TTWO/OptionChain/)), and Schaeffer's rated Take-Two options as richly priced against the stock's actual moves ([Finviz/Schaeffer's](https://finviz.com/news/423342/take-two-interactive-stock-slips-ahead-of-gta-vi-drop)). The earlier research backed out about 48% implied volatility from the $12.50 fill against realized volatility near 30%, and a roughly 16% implied chance of finishing above the $282.50 breakeven; those are modeled figures, not quotes. A trader who earns a fifth of a half-year's profit selling options into extremes is, by inference, more likely to have been a seller of that premium than a buyer.

On **sizing**, his framework demands a grade first. Lacking capitulation, a confirmed turn and a fresh surprise, this is not a top-grade setup on his variables, which means small size. The trade does satisfy his survival rule in one respect: a long call cannot lose more than its premium. It satisfies it fully only if losing the entire premium is tolerable. Finally, he would ask what the **exit plan** was at entry. Nothing in the investor's reasoning names one.

One more reading from his rules applies after the fact. He treats a stock that refuses to fall on bad news as a sign of a turn ([X, April 2022](https://x.com/TheOneLanceB/status/1516798713271767040)). The mirror image, which is my inference and not his statement, is that a stock closing down 3.67% on a 99 review score and sales estimates above the brokers' bars is a caution sign, softened by the fact that the broad market fell 2% that day and one report attributed a little over half the drop to it ([Nacionale](https://www.nacionale.com/en/articles/why-is-take-two-stock-down-today-gta-6-launch-day-ttwo-sell-the-news)).

### His prior-bar-low trail already fired on launch day

Translating his method requires choosing a bar size, and he has published none for multi-month trades. His statement that the concept holds on every chart supports using daily bars, and weekly bars for a longer hold. That choice is mine.

**On daily bars, the trail has triggered.** After the November 18 up day, the stop moves to that day's low of $210.50. Launch day traded through it and closed at $208.65. A trader following the rule mechanically sold on November 19 with the stock near $210, slightly above a $204-208 entry. His remarks about "selling under resistance" also suggest he would have sold part of the position into the launch-day spike to $225.68, which ran into the 200-day average and failed. So his method would most likely have him flat, or holding a small remainder.

**Right now, November 20**, the daily reference for anything still held is the launch-day low of **$208.20**. The pre-market quote of $211.86 is above it. A trade or close below $208.20 completes the exit on daily bars.

Daily bars are tight for a seven-month option when the stock's daily range has been above 8% of its price during launch week. The wider, structural version of his stop is the low of the move that the trade is leaning on. His stop belongs below the low of the capitulation, the point where the idea is wrong ([TradeZella](https://www.tradezella.com/strategies/mean-reversion-strategy)). On the bigger chart the V runs from the July high of $265.94 down to the October 30 low of $183.86 and back up to the November closing high of $231.31. The November 17 low near $203.93 is the first higher low on the right side of that V. **A break of $203.93 damages the higher-low structure; a break of $183.86 ends it.**

For **partial profits**, he has no fixed fractions. The inference from "hold the trend, or sell higher highs and buy back higher lows" is to sell portions into strength at resistance: first the 200-day average and launch-day high at $218-226, then the November high at $231.31, and eventually the area under the July high of $265.94. That last level matters for this contract. The $270 strike sits above the stock's all-time high, so a trader who sells under resistance would be selling the option while it is still out of the money. The earlier research modeled the call at about $26 with the stock at $260 in mid-February, and at about $10 with the stock at $260 after the May report.

For **re-entry or adding**, he would want a new confirmed turn: a daily close back above the 200-day average and then through $225.68, with the stop moved under the most recent higher low.

For the **February and May reports**, no rule of his was found. The expected-value frame implies treating each report as a new bet and asking whether the position would be opened fresh at that size on that day. His teaching that direction alone does not guarantee an option profit points to the specific hazard: implied volatility usually rises into a report and falls after it. By inference he would cut the position before each report to an amount he could lose entirely, then manage the reaction with the same rule, using the report day's low as the new stop after a gap up and exiting on a gap down that breaks the prior low.

## The two methods agree on the entry and split on the exit

| Question | Qullamaggie (inferred) | Breitstein (inferred) |
|---|---|---|
| Buy before a known event? | No. Buys after a surprise gap of 10% or more | No evidence he does. Prefers dislocations and breaking news |
| Buy a falling stock? | No. Entry is the opening range high on an up day | Only after capitulation and a confirmed turn |
| Options? | Does not trade them | Uses them tactically; watches implied volatility closely |
| Size | Loss capped at 0.25-1% of the account | Scales with setup grade; never large enough to cause psychological damage |
| Initial stop | Low of the entry day | Low of the move that defines the turn |
| Trail | First daily close below the 10- or 20-day average (20- or 50-day for news-driven moves) | Break of the prior bar's low on the chart being traded |
| Partial profits | A third to half after 3-5 days, then stop to breakeven | No fixed rule; sells into strength and under resistance when it maximizes expected value |
| Earnings | Hold only with a profit cushion and a partial sold | No stated rule; re-estimate expected value |
| Status on November 20 | Below the 10-day; only the 50-day at $207.64 remains | Daily trail triggered on Nov 19; $208.20 is the last daily reference |

The agreement is the more useful finding. Both men, working from opposite styles, require the market to confirm the idea before they commit money, and both define the exit from the stock's price structure at the moment of entry. Neither buys on a story about what a product launch ought to do. Both size so that a single loss is a non-event, and both make most of their money from a few large winners that they let run behind a trailing stop.

They part ways on four things. Qullamaggie is a momentum buyer who would not touch a pullback like this; Breitstein will buy a decline once it turns. Qullamaggie takes a fixed partial on a schedule; Breitstein rejects automatic profit-taking and wants each sale justified. Qullamaggie's trail is a moving average judged on the close, which tolerates intraday noise and is slow; Breitstein's is bar structure, which is fast and exits sooner. And Qullamaggie keeps risk nearly constant across trades, where Breitstein varies it sharply with quality. On this position the fast method has already exited and the slow method is one weak close from doing so.

## A workable trail for the option uses stock closes and a calendar

This section is my opinion, offered as a way to think about the scenarios.

**The trigger has to be the stock price, not the option price.** A long-dated, far out-of-the-money call trades with a wide gap between bid and ask and can be marked sharply lower on a quiet day, so a stop order resting on the option invites a bad fill at a price nobody intended. Both traders' rules are also written for the share price. The practical method is to watch the stock, decide on the daily close as Qullamaggie does, and then sell the option with a limit order.

**A stock trigger alone is not enough, because the option can lose while the chart holds.** The earlier research modeled this call with time decay of about $7 per contract per day and a loss of about $55 per contract for each one-point fall in implied volatility. In that model, a stock sitting at $206 leaves the option worth about $10.60 in mid-December and $6.15 in mid-February at unchanged volatility, and about $6.00 and $3.00 if implied volatility falls 10 points. The stock price needed just to keep the option at its $12.50 cost rises from about $212-229 in mid-December to $229-243 in mid-February and $266-272 after the May report. A stock that holds its 50-day average for three months is a success for a shareholder and a slow failure for this option. That is why a deadline has to sit beside the price stop.

With those two points in mind, a sensible plan blends the two methods as follows.

**On the downside, use two steps.** A daily close below the 50-day average and launch-day low, roughly $207.64-208.20, is the first warning from both methods and a reasonable point to sell a third to half. A daily close below $203.93 breaks the higher low and fills the November 18 gap, and is a reasonable point to sell the rest. The October 30 low of $183.86 is the level that ends the chart thesis, but it is useless as a stop for the option: the model values the call at only about $2-5 with the stock near $180 in mid-December, a loss of 60-85% of the premium before the stop is reached.

**On the upside, sell a first piece early.** A push into $222-231, the zone holding the 200-day average, the launch-day high and the November high, is where both methods would sell something: Qullamaggie on his 3-5 day rule and Breitstein under resistance. The model puts the call at about $15-19 there in mid-December at unchanged volatility, and near cost if volatility has fallen 10 points, so this sale is as much about reducing risk as banking gain. After that, once the stock is above a rising 10-day average, hold the remainder until the first daily close below it. Sell another piece under the July high, in the $255-266 area, without waiting for the strike.

**Set the deadline at the February report.** If the stock is not near or above about $230-240 in the week before the report, the option will be below cost with no cushion, and Qullamaggie's earnings rule says not to carry it through. Selling before the report also sells while implied volatility is typically elevated. If the stock is above that zone and a partial has been sold, carrying a reduced position through is consistent with both methods. The earlier research showed why the report cuts both ways: after Red Dead Redemption 2, the February 2019 report beat on bookings and the stock fell about 13% on the outlook ([GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/red-dead-redemption-2-gallops-to-23-million-copies-in-a-quarter-stock-falls-13/)).

**Treat May as a final sale date.** After the May report the option has about four weeks left and is close to all-or-nothing. Only contracts already paid for by earlier sales belong in that report, and anything left afterward should be sold within days unless the stock is above $270.

**Add one option-side check.** Because no post-launch quote or implied-volatility reading was found, the first step is to get both from a live chain, along with the real 10- and 20-day averages. A rough reading of the earlier model's Greeks puts the call somewhere around $8-14 with the stock at $209-212, depending on how far implied volatility has fallen since launch; that is my arithmetic and not a quote. A simple floor, such as reassessing the whole position if the option trades at half its cost while the stock is still above its stops, catches the volatility and decay losses that a stock trigger misses.

The strongest argument against this plan is that it is too active for a position meant to capture one large move, and that every early sale shrinks the payoff in the rare outcome where the stock runs to $300. That is true. It is also what both traders' rules accept: they give up some of the best case to make sure the common case costs little.

## Conclusion

The exercise changes the question the investor should be asking. The original reasoning was about the business: a record launch, a stock off its highs, earnings that look temporarily weak. Both traders would regard that as beside the point. Their rules care about whether the market has confirmed the idea, where the trade is proven wrong, and how much is lost when it is. On those tests the purchase was early and undefined, and the launch-day reversal has already used up most of the room either method allows. The business view can still turn out right; the Sensor Tower estimates suggest the launch itself beat expectations. The contract can still lose, because it needs a new all-time high inside seven months.

The practical lesson from setting the two methods side by side is that they converge on the same next step for different reasons. The setup both of them would buy has not happened yet: a surprise that gaps the stock 10% on heavy volume for Qullamaggie, or a confirmed higher low and reclaim of the 200-day average for Breitstein. An official sales figure or the February report could supply either. Until then, the position is best treated as a small stake with a written exit on the stock chart and a date on the calendar. Every level and option value here should be checked against a live quote and the actual trade confirmation, and none of this is personalized investment advice.
