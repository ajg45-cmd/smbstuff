# Programs stay hidden; conditions can be measured

None of the thirteen questions has an answer that lets him see a specific program, but most have an answer that tells him which conditions he is trading in, and several of his working assumptions need correcting. The most important correction is about impact: the evidence is that **about two-thirds of a finished order's peak impact is still in the price at that day's close (about one-third reverts)**, and it decays slowly toward roughly one-third of peak over about 50 days; price does not "mean revert two-thirds of the move". Second, the waterfall he fades in a turn trade is not a VWAP or TWAP order: under the square-root impact law a scheduled order cannot move a stock 1 ATR in 20 to 30 minutes, and the only direct study of such moves found an average rebound of about 15 to 20% of the drop, mostly inside 30 to 60 minutes. Third, the half-hour persistence result says the same clock window tends to repeat on following days by a few basis points relative to other stocks; within the same day the adjacent half-hour reverses. Fourth, a patient buyer's fills print at the bid and are counted as selling, so bid/ask delta measures urgency and not accumulation, and a 100-share tape filter hides more than 80% of prints in stocks above $250. Buybacks and unlocks are both real, rule-bound flows that are almost entirely invisible on the day: issuer buying is disclosed monthly, weeks after the quarter, and the measured unlock effect is about −1.5% over three days with a permanent volume step-up and no documented bounce. The practical output is a relative-volume pace gauge he can build, a news-day timeline, a tape-filter setting by price tier, and a rated playbook at the end.

**How to read the evidence labels.** **Documented** means peer-reviewed research, rule text, exchange or index-provider data. **Practice** means what a broker, law firm, vendor or named trader says is done, with no published test. **Unverified** means folklore, a single weak source, or a claim the research could not trace. **Own data** means his Notion records, which come from a simulated forward-test account and one-minute-bar replays, often with small samples ([Monthly Review Oct 2026](https://app.notion.com/p/40b821abbd81810ea134e1120162d42a)). Where I add arithmetic or reasoning of my own it is labelled **inference**.

**Source access, stated up front.** Nearly every source in this round was read only through search-result excerpts or abstracts, because direct page fetches were blocked. The research notes say only eight papers were read in full text, all on arXiv: Heston, Korajczyk and Sadka (half-hour periodicity); Bucci et al. 2019 (impact decay) and Bucci et al. on the linear-to-square-root crossover; Zarinelli et al.; Farmer, Gerig, Lillo and Waelbroeck; Zawadowski, Andor and Kertész (extreme intraday moves); Moro et al.; and Golub, Keane and Poon (mini flash crashes). Everything on buybacks, unlocks, news and institutional trading, odd lots, thinkorswim and the volume curve is excerpt-only, including the Rule 10b-18 text, which should be checked against eCFR before anyone quotes it. Books by Kissell and Johnson were not opened. The thinkScript below is an untested draft. Where sources disagree the disagreement is shown, not averaged.

## 1. A pace indicator measures volume conditions and cannot detect a program

**Plain answer.** He can build a time-of-day relative-volume indicator that compares today's cumulative volume with the average cumulative volume at the same clock time over the last N sessions. It tells him whether the market is trading faster or slower than its normal curve. It cannot tell him that a program has fallen behind, because the parent order's size, side, start time, end time and schedule are not observable by anyone outside the broker. It is a conditions gauge.

**Why the simple version is good enough.** The baseline used throughout the literature is "rolling means": average the volume in the same time bin over past days ([Chen, Feng and Palomar summary](https://alphatrade.readthedocs.io/en/latest/_volume_kalman.html)). Dynamic models beat it by 29% to 64% on raw volume forecasts but by only about 7% on the percent-of-day curve and 9% to 15% on VWAP tracking ([FlexTrade summary of Satish, Saxena and Palmer](https://flextrade.com/resources/predicting-intraday-trading-volume-and-volume-percentages/), [Chen et al.](https://alphatrade.readthedocs.io/en/latest/_volume_kalman.html)). The shape of the day is stable; the hard part is today's total. That matters because the brokers' own algorithms mostly use the simple version: "Most VWAP strategies run on a historical volume schedule with limited real-time consideration" ([Credit Suisse AES via GlobalTrading](https://www.globaltrading.net/?p=31156)). His gauge therefore approximates the curve the schedules were built from. All of this is **documented at excerpt level**.

**The formulas.** These follow TradingView's documented definition of "Relative Volume at Time", which takes the time offset from the session anchor and averages the same offset across a set number of prior sessions, in cumulative or per-bar mode ([TradingView help](https://in.tradingview.com/support/solutions/43000705489)).

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Cumulative relative volume at time t | RVOL(t) = CumVol_today(t) ÷ average over the last N days of CumVol_day(t) |
| Percent-of-day curve | p(t) = average over N days of [CumVol_day(t) ÷ TotalVol_day] |
| Projected end-of-day volume | Proj(t) = CumVol_today(t) ÷ p(t) |
| Pace against a normal day | Pace(t) = Proj(t) ÷ average daily volume over N days |

**Build spec (inference from the sources above, not a tested design).** Compute per symbol from its own history; do not use one market-wide curve. Use regular-hours bars only, with five-minute bins as a reasonable compromise. Use N = 20 sessions; a community script claims TradingView defaults to 5, which is too few ([TradingView community script](https://id.tradingview.com/script/3Zv6IuBl-Intraday-RVOL-Overlay-Time-Of-Day-Relative-Volume/)). Use a median or trimmed mean across days, and drop earnings days, option expirations, index rebalance days and half days from the lookback; Chen et al. needed an explicit outlier-robust version, which supports this. Keep the closing auction out of the continuous curve: the auction's share of consolidated volume swung between 5.19% and 8.55% across months of 2025 alone ([Rosenblatt Oct 2025](https://www.rblt.com/market-structure-reports/us-securities-volumes-october-2025), [Rosenblatt Nov 2025](https://www.rblt.com/market-structure-reports/us-securities-volumes-november-2025)). Treat readings before about 10:00 as noisy, because p(t) is small early and a small error in it becomes a large error in projected volume.

**thinkorswim limits.** Intraday aggregations cannot span more than 360 days and tick aggregations more than 5 days ([thinkorswim manual](https://tlc.thinkorswim.com/center/howToTos/thinkManual/charts/Chart-Aggregation/Setting-up-Chart-Time-Frame)). A script cannot reference an aggregation lower than the chart's own ([thinkScript tutorial ch. 11](https://toslc.thinkorswim.com/center/reference/thinkScript/tutorials/Advanced/Chapter-11---Referencing-Secondary-Aggregation)). So the study must run on the intraday chart itself, and N is capped by how many days that chart loads. The community RVOL scripts the research found divide by a daily average or a moving average of bars, which is not time-of-day normalised, and no complete, verified thinkScript for the cumulative time-of-day version was found ([usethinkscript RVOL thread](https://usethinkscript.com/threads/rvol-relative-volume-indicator-watchlist-for-thinkorswim.1014)). The draft below comes from the research notes and has not been run. It assumes extended hours are off, no half days are in the window, and every bar exists; a missing bar in a thin stock or a 1:00 pm close shifts the fixed offset so "same bar N days ago" is no longer the same clock time. Check it against a hand calculation before trusting it.

```
# Cumulative RVOL by time of day - UNTESTED DRAFT
# Intraday chart, extended hours OFF, lookback days loaded on the chart.
input days = 20;
def barsPerDay = RoundDown(23400000 / GetAggregationPeriod(), 0);  # 390 min RTH
def newDay = GetYYYYMMDD() != GetYYYYMMDD()[1];
def cumVol = if newDay then volume else cumVol[1] + volume;
def sumPrior = fold i = 1 to days + 1 with s = 0
               do s + GetValue(cumVol, i * barsPerDay);
def avgPrior = sumPrior / days;
plot RVOLcum = if avgPrior > 0 then cumVol / avgPrior else Double.NaN;
plot One = 1;
```

**What each reading means.** The first column uses his own thresholds, because his turn data already split on them: relative volume below 1.2 won 35% at −0.38R (n=23), 1.2 to 2 won 58% at −0.10R (n=32), and 2 or more won 50% at +0.37R (n=40), a result his page marks as directional only (p ≈ 0.12) ([RVOL study](https://app.notion.com/p/418821abbd8181f3a591d2d696db240e)). He should confirm that study used a time-of-day measure before treating the two as the same number.

| Reading | What it documents | What it implies for scheduled orders, if any exist (inference) | What it does not say |
|---|---|---|---|
| Below about 1.2 | The stock is not trading more than normal | A fixed-quantity VWAP or TWAP order is a larger share of each bin than planned, has fewer passive fills available, and is more likely to cross the spread to keep schedule. A percent-of-volume order has done fewer shares than its owner expected and carries more into the afternoon | That any order exists, its side, or that the close will be heavier. "Light midday means a catch-up close" has no study behind it |
| 1.2 to 2 | Elevated participation | Schedules built on the historical curve are roughly on plan | Direction |
| 2 or more | In play. After earnings the next session runs "well over double normal" and volume stays elevated about a week ([Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/earnings-announcements-sliced-and-diced)) | Percent-of-volume orders finish early and stop. A static-curve VWAP under-trades the front-loaded morning and over-trades later relative to true VWAP | Whether the extra volume is institutional. Whether the buyer or seller is done |
| Pace falling through the day after a high open | The opening burst is fading | Urgent flow is finishing; whatever continues is slower | That the move is over |

The documented behaviour behind the third column is narrow. A historical-schedule VWAP order "will go right ahead to post and subsequently cross the spread to keep to schedule" ([Credit Suisse AES](https://www.globaltrading.net/?p=31156)), and a time-variant percent-of-volume order raises its rate when it lags, in one broker example from 1.00% to 3.32% by midday ([IBKR POV lesson](https://www.interactivebrokers.com/campus/trading-lessons/percentage-of-volume/)). The one study showing that VWAP child orders are predictable used more than 20,000 parent orders and the broker's own child-order records, which the public does not have ([Sağlam, Financial Management 2020](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:finmgt:v:49:y:2020:i:1:p:33-67)). No source quantifies tracking-band widths or how often orders finish behind schedule. The only public feed that carries actual order information is the closing-auction imbalance after its release time.

## 2. Interval VWAP is the order's own VWAP, and round times draw volume without documented reversals

**Plain answer.** Interval VWAP is the volume-weighted average price over the life of one order, from the moment the desk enters the market to the moment it finishes. The "interval" is that order's own start-to-end window, so it is different for every order and is not a fixed clock interval. It does not imply reversals at 10:00 or 10:30. What is documented is a roughly 20% bump in volume and trade count at round clock marks; reversal clustering at those times is not documented anywhere the research could find.

**The definition.** "An all-day VWAP would measure the average price from the open to the close ... Traders more often use interval VWAP, which looks at the specific trading interval, from the time they enter the market to the time they exit" ([Northern Trust](https://www.northerntrust.com/united-states/insights-research/2026/asset-servicing/its/trade-execution-benchmarks)). Talos defines market VWAP as "the interval VWAP ... across the client parent order time duration" ([Talos](https://www.talos.com/insights/execution-insights-through-transaction-cost-analysis-tca-benchmarks-and-slippage)). The consequence for his chart is an inference but a firm one: an order that starts at 10:47 is judged against a VWAP anchored at 10:47, so the session VWAP line is at best an approximation of the level any desk is measured against. Bloomberg adds that interval VWAP ignores order timing, and that switching the benchmark from VWAP to arrival price flips an order between outperforming and underperforming 41% of the time ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/professional/?p=47069)).

**What round times do.** In Nanex data on 30 liquid US stocks and 3 ETFs from 2003 to 2006, volume and number of trades spiked by about 20% at regular 5 or 10 minute intervals, at hourly, half-hourly and smaller round marks, with no significant change in transaction-cost measures during the spikes ([Broussard and Nikiforov](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:intfin:v:30:y:2014:i:c:p:196-204)). The authors blamed humans setting algorithms to round times; a CFA Institute digest called that speculative ([CFA digest](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/cfa-digest/2014/07/intraday-periodicity-in-algorithmic-trading-digest-summary)). A reporting artifact points the same way: a large block of institutional orders in one database were all stamped 9:30 to 4:20 and had to be discarded as placeholders ([Zarinelli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.2152)). This is **documented** for activity, on a sample twenty years old with no later replication found.

**What is not shown.** No study found tests reversal frequency by clock minute, and none gives the share of parent orders that start or stop on the hour. Activity spikes with unchanged costs do not imply turning points. One hard clock event is the NYSE closing-auction freeze at 15:50, when market-on-close and limit-on-close entry ends and the imbalance is published ([NYSE fact sheet](https://www.nyse.com/publicdocs/nyse/NYSE_Auctions_Closing_Process_Fact_Sheet.pdf)). Scheduled economic releases are a plausible second reason for turns near round times, but the notes could not verify release times against agency calendars, so he should check those himself. His mentor's turn window ("great until like 10:40, first 20 min is best") is **practice**, and a mechanism that fits it is the end of a price concession when an order stops, which is covered in section 7 ([Turn Trade](https://app.notion.com/p/3f8821abbd818198bb2bca504f2b60e9)).

## 3. No one can give odds that a program is active, but liquidity tells him where they live

**Plain answer.** There is no published probability that a VWAP or TWAP program is active in a given stock at a given moment, and no public breakdown of algorithm use by market cap. In a liquid large cap the honest answer is "almost always, from several owners at once, on both sides", which makes the question uninformative. In sub-$300 million low floats nothing found shows institutional schedule-based algorithms operating at all. Visually a program is mostly resting bids or offers, not aggressive prints.

**The survey figures are respondent shares.** In The TRADE's 2024 long-only survey 79% of respondents used VWAP algorithms and 77% used dark liquidity-seeking algorithms; a 2020 hedge-fund table showed about 62% for VWAP ([The TRADE long-only 2024](https://www.thetradenews.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Algo-survey.pdf), [The TRADE hedge funds 2020](https://www.thetradenews.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/TT-2020-Q2-Algo.pdf)). The earlier report quoted 83% of hedge funds from the 2026 survey ([The TRADE HF 2026](https://www.thetradenews.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Algo-Survey-HF-2026.pdf)). These count firms that use the type at all. None is a share of volume, and no source gives the share of institutional flow by algorithm type. This is **practice-level survey evidence**.

**Large, liquid names.** In one sample stock (AAPL, March to April 2008) there was "almost always an active metaorder" at any minute from a database that covers only about 5% of daily volume in the top 20 stocks, and overlapping orders tended to share a sign ([Zarinelli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.2152)). That database covers roughly 8% of CRSP dollar volume and about 10% of institutional activity, mostly pension plans and mutual funds ([Hu et al.](https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/bitstream/10397/80215/1/Hu_Institutional_Trading_Abel.pdf)). The inference is that in a mega-cap the useful question is the net imbalance of many small orders, which is not observable, and not whether one exists.

**Micro-caps and low floats.** Nasdaq research using off-exchange volume as a retail proxy found retail activity concentrated in microcaps and read lower turnover there as institutions being underweight ([Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/what-types-of-stocks-do-retail-investors-trade-2019-08-08)). Larger mutual funds deliberately hold bigger stocks to avoid trading costs ([Busse, Chordia, Jiang and Tang](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:67:y:2021:i:2:p:1227-1248)), and realised impact is roughly twice as high in small caps as in large caps ([Swedroe summary of Frazzini, Israel and Moskowitz](https://www.etf.com/sections/index-investor-corner/swedroe-trading-cost-truths)). An industry guide puts institutional holdings in micro-caps under 20% against 70 to 80% in large caps, without primary data, so that figure is **unverified** ([TitanFX](https://research.titanfx.com/us-stock-trading/micro-cap)). The VWAP line on a low-float runner is therefore a retail and market-maker reference. His own low-float study agrees with treating it as a state and not a program footprint: below VWAP another leg came about 1 time in 20, and no mechanical long on the hold was positive ([Low-float VWAP study](https://app.notion.com/p/3fb821abbd8181a1b2abfad24c21d8d2)).

**What a program looks like.** The measured fill mix for a standard VWAP algorithm was **65.3% passive, 21.9% midpoint and 12.57% aggressive** ([Themis Trading, citing an ITG-based study](https://blog.themistrading.com/?p=6148)). The average algorithmic parent in the largest sample tried to trade $287,000 over 84 minutes at 4.80% of volume, with fewer than 0.40% of child orders sent as market orders ([NBER conference paper](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f145256/f145256.pdf)). Child orders run in "strategic runs which oscillate between the aggressive or passive side of the spread" (same paper), and broker schedules in the institutional data are front-loaded, not flat ([Zarinelli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.2152)). So a buy program's passive fills print at the bid, roughly one fill in eight lifts an offer, and the visible picture is a steady share of volume with small bursts of aggression when it lags. Any quiet, persistent participant produces the same picture.

## 4. Machines trade the headline in seconds; institutions follow the same day and for days after

**Plain answer.** Both things are true at different horizons. Automated subscribers to machine-readable news reprice the stock within milliseconds to seconds. For earnings, most of the price adjustment happens after hours and pre-market through quote changes on thin volume. Institutions as a group then trade in the direction of the news starting right after the first release, with the largest share of their earnings-related trading in the days after the announcement. Whether prices keep drifting for weeks is disputed.

| Stage | Who is active | What is documented | Rating |
|---|---|---|---|
| Milliseconds to 5 seconds | Low-latency news-feed subscribers and market makers repricing quotes | Index products respond to macro surprises within 5 ms; profits to being fast are small, about $19,000 per event in SPY ([Chordia, Green and Kottimukkalur](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v31y2018i12p4650-4687..html)). For company newswire items, a larger share of the 120-second reaction lands in the first 5 seconds when the item is high-relevance, and the authors read an initial overreaction that slower traders correct ([von Beschwitz, Keim and Massa](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rasset/v10y2020i1p122-178..html)) | Documented. Vendors sell these feeds "for automated trading" ([Bloomberg EDF](https://www.bloomberg.com/professional/products/data/enterprise-catalog/event-driven-feeds/), [LSEG MRN](https://lseg.com/en/data-analytics/financial-news-service/machine-readable-news)): practice |
| Overnight and pre-market | Liquidity providers moving quotes; few trades | 99.1% of earnings are announced outside regular hours; surprises reach prices mostly through quote changes; a median of 16 after-hours trades ([Grégoire and Martineau](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3060094)). After-hours jump probability on earnings exceeds 90% ([Christensen et al.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X25000182)). Identified news explains 49.6% of overnight idiosyncratic volatility against 12.4% in trading hours ([Boudoukh et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v32y2019i3p992-1033..html)) | Documented |
| Open to about 10:00 | Opening auction absorbs the overnight imbalance; residual price discovery | Price discovery after earnings is "generally complete by 10 a.m." ([Martineau thesis](https://open.library.ubc.ca/cIRcle/collections/ubctheses/24/items/1.0348752)). The claim that the open carries 30 to 40% of the day's volume on a surprise comes from one education page and is unverified | Documented at thesis level |
| Rest of Day 1 | Institutions trading on news tone; elevated volume all day | Institutions "chiefly trade on the tone of news directly after the earliest news release in a cluster" and do not anticipate unscheduled news ([Huang, Tan and Wermers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:33:y:2020:i:10:p:4627-4675.)). Cumulative regular-hours returns after earnings are "mostly flat" on average ([Grégoire and Martineau](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-679X.12394)) | Documented |
| Days 2 to 5 | Institutional follow-through; analyst revisions | Institutional net trading tracks the surprise before, during and after, with the strongest relation and the largest share of trading in the post-announcement period ([JRFM, Ancerno 2002-2010](https://doi.org/10.3390/jrfm19040294)). Volume "stays elevated for around a week" ([Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/earnings-announcements-sliced-and-diced)) | Documented |
| Days 5 to 60 | Multi-day parent orders finishing; slower holders | Drift is concentrated where institutional attention was low ([Ben-Rephael, Da and Israelsen](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:30:y:2017:i:9:p:3009-3047.)). Its current size is disputed, below | Documented but contested |

**The drift dispute.** Martineau's 2022 paper says prices now fully reflect earnings surprises on the announcement date and that drift has "been non-existent since 2006" for large stocks and only recently disappeared for microcaps ([Critical Finance Review](https://ideas.repec.org/a/now/jnlcfr/104.00000122.html)). A 2025 UCLA Anderson brief reports two accepted papers that find drift "alive and well" and a working paper that blames research design for the disagreement ([UCLA Anderson Review](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/is-post-earnings-announcement-drift-a-thing-again)). Non-peer-reviewed recent estimates point in opposite directions. I take no side beyond this: where drift survives it is in smaller, less-followed names, which is the opposite of the liquid in-play names he trades.

**What this means for Day 1.** Being first to a headline belongs to machines; nothing found supports a human edge in that window. Nothing found supports the folklore that long-only institutions fade Day 1 gaps; in aggregate they trade with the news. Because the average regular-hours return after earnings is flat, there is no average intraday drift to lean on, and any Day 1 trend is variance around zero that has to be confirmed by price and volume. No peer-reviewed large-sample study of gap-and-go against gap-fade on earnings days was found. The closest test is a backtest of a five-minute opening-range breakout on high-relative-volume "stocks in play" that reports a Sharpe ratio of 2.81 for 2016 to 2023; it is not peer-reviewed, its authors have a commercial day-trading affiliation, and its cost assumptions were not checked, so it stays **unverified** ([Zarattini, Barbon and Aziz](https://ideas.repec.org/p/chf/rpseri/rp2498.html)). SMB's "changing fundamentals" and second-day plays are attributed **practice** with no published test ([MoneyShow](https://www.moneyshow.com/articles/daytraders-42251)).

**How programs behave on the day.** No broker or transaction-cost study of event-day execution was retrieved. The reasoning that a historical-curve VWAP mis-schedules on a front-loaded news day, and that desks switch to participation or arrival-price logic, is **practice** and inference. His own records fit the documented timeline better than they fit a Day 1 program story: his Day 2 flush-turn variant has 96 trades at a profit factor of 1.44 to 1.54 ([Turn Trade](https://app.notion.com/p/3f8821abbd818198bb2bca504f2b60e9)), and his DKS note that the forced seller "finished in one session" while the discretionary buyer "had legs" over three days on falling volume is his own statement of the Days 2 to 5 row ([Day 2](https://app.notion.com/p/38d821abbd8181cfbdb8ff94ac1483e9)). His Day 2 lane has nine cases, which is too few to size on.

## 5. The 100-share filter is harmless in cheap stocks and blinding above $250

**Plain answer.** It depends on price. In stocks under roughly $20 to $50 the filter removes little volume and does simplify the tape. Above $250 it hides more than four prints in five and, since November 2025, hides trades that are now official round lots and set the quote. In mid- and high-priced names it biases his tape away from institutional child orders, which is the opposite of what the filter is usually meant to do.

**What is hidden, by count.** Odd lots were 57% of all US equity trades in January 2023 and **66% in August 2025**. In stocks above $250 they were **83.4% of trades** in February 2025; in sub-$1 stocks, 29.0% ([MEMX](https://memx.com/insights/equity-adv-option-contracts-and-odd-lot-trades-all-up-year-over-year)). MEMX states that odd-lot share "is not a great measure of retail activity" ([MEMX retail insights](https://memx.com/insights/retail-trading-insights)).

**What is hidden, by volume.** The only sourced volume figures are from 2021: odd lots were about 6.3% of executed share volume on Cboe's exchanges and about 10.7% in the most liquid stocks, rising with price ([Cboe](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/an-in-depth-view-into-odd-lots)), and under 30% of shares in high-priced stocks ([Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/odd-facts-about-odd-lots-2021-04-22)). No 2024 to 2026 volume figure was found, and since the trade-count share has risen the true numbers are probably higher. The table combines the sourced counts with those dated volume figures; the volume column is an estimate.

| Price tier | Prints hidden by a 100-share filter | Volume hidden (estimate from 2021 data) | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under $1 to low single digits | About 30% | Very little | Filter simplifies; little lost |
| $20 to $250 | Roughly half to two-thirds | Single digits to low teens percent | Usable, but absorption at a level is undercounted |
| $250.01 to $1,000 | More than 80% | "Under 30%" in 2021, plausibly more now | Hurts. The round lot is now 40 shares |
| Above $1,000 | More than 80% | As above | Hurts most. The round lot is 10 shares and average inside size is about 32 shares |

**The round-lot redefinition.** The SIPs implemented new round-lot tiers in November 2025, based on average closing price: 100 shares up to $250, 40 shares from $250.01 to $1,000, 10 shares from $1,000.01 to $10,000, and 1 share above that ([Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/new-round-lot-rules-help-issuers-high-priced-stocks), [SEC order 34-104612](https://sec.gov/files/rules/exorders/2026/34-104612.pdf)). In the $1,000 to $10,000 group average inside size fell from about 99 to about 32 shares ([Cboe](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/the-impact-round-lot-reform-had-on-u-s-equities-market-quality)). Industry notices say odd-lot quotes at or better than the NBBO have been on the SIP since 27 April 2026 ([Cboe notice](https://www.cboe.com/notices/content/?id=59495)). A "100-share minimum" in a $600 stock is therefore a 2.5-round-lot minimum.

**The bias.** When odd lots were off the tape entirely, a study of 120 Nasdaq stocks found a median 24% of trades missing and attributed odd lots to algorithms slicing orders; those trades were highly informative ([O'Hara, Yao and Ye](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:69:y:2014:i:5:p:2199-2236)). A small trade is now far more likely to be a slice of a large institutional order, so size is an unreliable marker in both directions ([NBER conference paper](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f145256/f145256.pdf)). A 100-share print is not an institution and a 7-share print is not retail. The inference for his setup: keep the filter on names under about $50, lower it to the round-lot size (40 or 10) above $250, and in those names read absorption from volume-at-price per bar and not from filtered prints. His October results in $200-plus names were −$4,148 ([Monthly Review Oct 2026](https://app.notion.com/p/40b821abbd81810ea134e1120162d42a)); nothing shows the filter caused that, but those are the names where his tape is thinnest. One further caution from the notes: very large prints on the trade-reporting facility often carry modifiers such as weighted-average price, whose price "may bear no relation to the current market" ([FINRA](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/notices/trade-reporting-notice-081910)), so a 10,000-share filter is not a clean institutional tape either.

## 6. A passive buyer prints at the bid, so the second description is closer and still not a signature

**Plain answer.** Of his two pictures, "most volume transacts at the bid, the bid does not drop, and the price walks up" matches the documented mechanics of a patient buyer. "Price breaks the bid then reclaims" has no documented link to program buying. Neither is a verified signature, because heavy volume at a bid that holds is equally the picture of a seller hitting a bid that is about to give way.

**Why the fills print at the bid.** A passive buyer rests a bid and is filled only when someone sells to it. The print is at the bid, and every trade-signing rule marks it as a sell. With the measured VWAP fill mix of 65.3% passive and 21.9% midpoint, only about one fill in eight shows as a buy at the ask ([Themis Trading](https://blog.themistrading.com/?p=6148)). The mechanism follows from the definitions; no paper was found that measures how often passive institutional buys are tagged as sells in public data, so the proportion is inference.

**What delta measures.** Signing rules identify who took liquidity. They classified 76.4% to 81.05% of Nasdaq trades correctly in the classic test, with "only a very limited success in classifying trades executed inside the quotes" ([Ellis, Michaely and O'Hara](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/accuracy-of-trade-classification-rulesevidence-from-nasdaq/A2CAD2206B562784527548F5283A9ECB)), and misclassify up to about 31% of trades in later work ([Chakrabarty, Moulton and Shkilko](https://sha.cornell.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/4/2019/04/ShortsLongsandLeeReady_JFMforthcoming.pdf)). Off-exchange trading was about half of 2025 volume and prints largely inside the quote ([Cboe](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/2025-u-s-equities-year-in-review)). **Cumulative delta is therefore a measure of net urgency, not net accumulation.** Negative delta with a price that will not fall is consistent with absorption; it is not proof of it. Those accuracy figures come from 2000 to 2012 data, and no figure for today's market was found.

**Reserve-order mechanics.** Exchange rules confirm the "held bid" mechanism. On Nasdaq, when the displayed part of a reserve order falls below a round lot, "a new displayed order will be entered and receive a new timestamp, while the size of the non-displayed order will be reduced by the same amount" ([Federal Register, Nasdaq Rule 4703(h)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2021-02-18/pdf/2021-03214.pdf)). NYSE-family rules say the same: each replenishment gets a new working time ([Federal Register, NYSE American](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2020/03/18/2020-05553/self-regulatory-organizations-nyse-american-llc-notice-of-filing-of-proposed-rule-change-amending)). On the tape that is many prints at one price, cumulative volume at that price well beyond the size ever shown, and displayed size snapping back after each fill.

**What cannot be distinguished.** The same picture comes from a fully hidden order, several participants each re-posting, a market maker requoting, or an algorithm sending a fresh child order after each fill. The tape does not say which resting order was hit, trades at that price on other venues are mixed in, a held bid shows nothing about remaining size, and it can vanish on the next print. No source was found on the accuracy of iceberg detection from public data. With his 100-share filter on, selling into a bid in a high-priced stock arrives largely as odd lots and is undercounted. The safe-harbor rule adds one firm fact for buyback names: a compliant issuer purchase cannot be priced above the highest independent bid or the last independent trade, so issuer flow never initiates an uptick (section 11).

His own records already treat this correctly. The held bid is logged as an entry with the dropped bid as the exit, and AMD on 11/11 lost $204 when the 530 bid broke four minutes after entry ([11/11 notes](https://app.notion.com/p/415821abbd818085875ee4a815fd41bd)). The pattern is **practice** with no published hit rate.

## 7. Two-thirds of the impact stays; one-third reverts by the close

**Plain answer, and a correction.** He wrote that price "mean reverts 2/3 of the move" after an order finishes. The evidence says the reverse. **On average about two-thirds of the peak impact is still in the price at the same day's close, so about one-third has reverted.** Over the following weeks the remainder decays slowly, toward roughly one-third of peak at about 50 days. Expect a partial give-back, not a round trip.

**The measurements.** In about 8 million single-day institutional orders in Russell 3000 stocks from 2007 to mid-2010, the ratio of start-to-close impact to peak impact averaged **0.66 ± 0.04**, and "relaxation takes place as soon as the metaorder ends" ([Bucci et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05332)). Impact at the next day's close was about four-fifths of impact at the execution-day close. After removing the effect of follow-on orders in the same direction, the fit converges at about 50 days to roughly half of the day-one-close level, which is about one-third of peak, with large error bars (same paper). A second study of 6.9 million orders followed for up to three times the order's duration found the price settling "remarkably close to (though slightly higher than) 2/3 of the peak impact" ([Zarinelli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.2152)). London and Madrid data show reversion to 0.5 to 0.7 of peak ([Moro et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/0908.0202)).

**Where sources conflict.** The "fair pricing" theory predicts that two-thirds of peak impact is permanent, and a study of large US buy-side orders supports it ([Farmer et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1102.5457), [Bershova and Rakhlin](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:quantf:v:13:y:2013:i:11:p:1759-1778)). Bucci et al. find "no support" for a two-thirds permanent level once the multi-day decay is followed, and cite one manager's orders decaying to about 0.15 of initial impact after 15 days. Both camps agree on the same-day figure. They disagree on where it ends up weeks later, which matters for a swing trader and hardly at all for him.

**Why, in plain language.** While a large buyer is working, sellers only supply stock at a worsening price. Part of that price rise is a temporary concession, a fee the buyer pays for liquidity. Part is information: the market infers that someone large wants the stock. When the buyer stops, the concession starts to fade and the information stays. The fair-pricing argument explains the size of the fade: liquidity providers break even only if the price after the order is finished equals the average price the order paid, and since the order paid less than the peak on its early fills, the price must fall back from the peak to that average. With the order-size distribution the authors assume, that works out to a one-third reversion ([Farmer et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1102.5457)).

**How fast the first part goes.** The decay runs in volume time, measured as z, the market volume traded after the order ends divided by the volume traded while it ran. The values below are the research notes' arithmetic from the fitted Bucci curve, so they are inference from a documented fit, and they are averages over millions of orders with very wide dispersion.

| Volume traded since the order ended (z) | 0.1 | 0.25 | 0.5 | 1 | 2 | 5 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Share of peak impact remaining | 0.91 | 0.85 | 0.79 | 0.72 | 0.64 | 0.54 | 0.47 |

For an order that ran 30 minutes, that is about 10% of the push back in the first few minutes, 20 to 30% after another 15 to 30 minutes of comparable volume, and about a third once twice the order's volume has traded. The first minutes are the fastest in rate but small in size. Long, low-participation orders relax more slowly, which the authors attribute to their end being harder to detect; for the highest participation rates impact "relaxes toward zero" in the window studied ([Zarinelli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.2152)). An independent estimate for ordinary liquidity shocks says the market takes "longer than 30 minutes, but less than 60 minutes" to restore them ([Heston, Korajczyk and Sadka](https://arxiv.org/abs/1005.3535)).

**Informed against cash-flow orders.** During execution, impact looks the same whether the order carries information or not. Afterwards they separate: for trades labelled as cash flows, "the impact reverts almost completely on average in two to five days" ([Gomes and Waelbroeck](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/quantf/v15y2015i5p773-793.html)). The Bucci authors' reading is that a truly informed order should show no decay, an uninformed one should decay to zero, and the observed average is a blend. The tape does not reveal which kind he is watching. News is the best proxy he has: flow behind a fundamental headline should retrace least.

**Do not confuse this with his own statistic.** His hold-to-VWAP page says "fast snaps fade back two-thirds of the time" ([Hold to VWAP study](https://app.notion.com/p/418821abbd8181e9ab6ff7b5b9adb397)). That is a frequency in his own 17 fast-snap trades. It is not the size of impact reversion, and the two numbers should not be merged. All the impact datasets are institutional orders at under 30% participation worked by scheduling algorithms; applying the one-third figure to a panic waterfall is extrapolation.

## 8. Most orders are short and same-day; the long tail is what persists

**Plain answer.** By count, the typical institutional order is short: a median of about 35 minutes in one dataset, an 84-minute average in another, and 92.5% finished the day they started in a third. Stitched across days and brokers, the average parent runs 1.38 trading days and roughly 30% span more than one day. These numbers do not conflict. They count different things.

| Statistic | What was counted | Sample | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Median 35 minutes; median about 0.09 of the day's volume clock | One broker ticket on one day | ANcerno institutional tickets, 2007 to 2010 | [Bucci et al., crossover paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.05230) |
| Average 84 minutes, $287,000, 4.80% of volume | Algorithmic parent orders at the execution-algorithm level | 2.3 million parents, 2012 to 2016 | [NBER conference paper](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f145256/f145256.pdf) |
| 92.5% completed the same day | Institutional orders by count | Breen, Hodrick and Korajczyk (2002), as cited | [Heston, Korajczyk and Sadka](https://arxiv.org/abs/1005.3535) |
| Average 1.38 trading days and 1.74 brokers | Parent orders reconstructed by stitching tickets across days and brokers | ANcerno | [Splitting and Shuffling](https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2021/preliminary/paper/ibSfSdEb) |
| About 30% multi-day | Same reconstruction (figure from the earlier report; this round could not re-retrieve the duration table) | ANcerno | [Splitting and Shuffling](https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2021/preliminary/paper/ibSfSdEb) |

**Reconciling them.** A fund's decision to buy a million shares over four days appears as four or more tickets, each of which looks like a short same-day order in the ticket-level data. The filtered ticket samples also exclude anything not finished in one day by construction. Counting by number of orders weights thousands of small orders equally with the few large ones; by dollars the picture shifts toward the long tail. Duration and participation rate are both distributed as truncated power laws spanning orders of magnitude, so there is no "normal" duration, only a median with a very fat tail ([Zarinelli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.2152)). Order direction is autocorrelated over many days, so the same side tends to return ([Bucci et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05332)). All of this is from 2007 to 2016 data; nothing was found for the 2020s.

**What changes duration.** Size relative to daily volume is the most important price-impact variable in live execution data, and patient managers work passive orders to avoid paying it ([Frazzini, Israel and Moskowitz](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3229719)). Manager identity and style matter: classic studies treat multi-day "packages" as the unit for large managers and find duration varies with style and motive ([Chan and Lakonishok](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:50:y:1995:i:4:p:1147-74), [Keim and Madhavan](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:37:y:1995:i:3:p:371-398)). Urgency trades off against detection: in European data, institutions appear to balance speed of profit capture against being spotted ([van Kervel and Menkveld](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:74:y:2019:i:3:p:1091-1137)). Very aggressive orders are typically short and long orders more often have low participation ([Zarinelli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.2152)). News is arithmetic: with volume more than double normal, an order of fixed size and participation cap finishes faster on Day 1. No study was found that measures duration conditional on news, earnings or volatility, and "a fund takes days to weeks to build a position after news" is **practice** consistent with the tail, not a measured fact.

**Managing expectations.** He should assume that any single order behind a move is more likely to end within the hour than to last the day, that he cannot know which kind he is watching, and that persistence across a session usually comes from several same-direction orders overlapping, which is documented as herding in the AAPL sample. All-day orders exist but are "a small bump" in the duration distribution ([Zarinelli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.2152)). The actionable form is his existing rule: stay with one-sided flow while it visibly continues, and treat a volume fade with a weaker bid or offer as the cue that the order has ended and a one-third give-back is the average expectation.

## 9. The waterfall is urgency and thin liquidity, and the documented bounce is small and fast

**Plain answer.** No. A move of 1 ATR away from VWAP in 20 to 30 minutes is far too large to be one VWAP or TWAP order. It takes news, urgent selling at very high participation, several same-direction orders at once, or liquidity providers stepping back, usually in combination. The one direct study of such moves found an average rebound of about 15 to 20% of the drop, most of it in the first 10 to 30 minutes and nothing further after an hour.

**The arithmetic (inference from the documented square-root law).** Impact is approximately daily volatility times the square root of order size over daily volume, with a constant near one ([Zarinelli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.2152)). A full-day VWAP order for 10% of daily volume has expected total impact of about 0.32 of one day's volatility, spread over six and a half hours. In any 20-minute midday window it trades about 0.3 to 0.5% of the day's volume, an incremental push of roughly 0.05 to 0.07 of daily volatility. Producing one full daily volatility of impact needs an order near a full day's volume, which cannot trade in 20 minutes at scheduled participation rates. ATR is larger than close-to-close volatility, which widens the gap.

**What does cause it.** The evidence here is thinner than the lore. Mini flash crashes are attributed to aggressive sweep orders hitting thin books: of 5,140 such events in four volatile months, 67.85% were classified as initiated by intermarket sweep orders ([Golub, Keane and Poon](https://arxiv.org/abs/1211.6667)). High-frequency traders on average supply liquidity during single-stock extreme moves and absorb imbalances created by others, but demand liquidity when several stocks move at once ([Brogaard et al.](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:128:y:2018:i:2:p:253-265)). Institutional sell herding is documented ([Zarinelli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.2152)). Stop cascades, forced liquidation and option-hedging flows are the usual explanations on a desk; no source opened quantifies them for single-stock flushes, so they remain plausible and **unverified** in size.

**The rebound table.** The study used liquid NYSE and Nasdaq stocks from 2000 to 2002. An event was a move within 60 minutes exceeding both 4% and eight times the stock's normal 60-minute volatility for that time of day, excluding the first 5 and last 60 minutes of the session ([Zawadowski, Andor and Kertész](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0406696)).

| Sample | Average move | Rebound, 0 to 10 min | 0 to 30 min | 0 to 60 min | 60 to 120 min |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| NYSE drops (222 events) | −7.2% | +0.84% | +1.13% | +1.13% | +0.01% (not significant) |
| Nasdaq drops (215) | −11.0% | +1.06% | +1.83% | +2.15% | +0.17% (not significant) |
| NYSE rises (175) | +3.1% | −0.73% | −0.73% | −0.63% | −0.12% (not significant) |
| Nasdaq rises (273) | +5.3% | −1.24% | −1.51% | −1.68% | not reported |

Rebound size rose roughly linearly with the size of the move. On the NYSE the spread widened so much that it removed most contrarian profit; on Nasdaq, buying at the ask and selling at the bid 60 minutes later earned 2.686% over 159 events. The caveats are heavy. The sample is over twenty years old, the events are much more extreme than a 1 ATR flush, the clock starts at the end of the move, which no live trader knows in advance, and the authors did not separate news from no-news events. No modern US study of rebounds after 20 to 30 minute moves, and none scaled by ATR, was found.

**News against no news.** At daily horizons, large moves without identifiable news tend to reverse and moves with news tend to drift ([Chan](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v70y2003i2p223-260.html), [Savor](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:106:y:2012:i:3:p:635-659)), and reversal is weaker on news days ([Tetlock](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:23:y:2010:i:9:p:3520-3557)). Returns to short-term reversal rise with the VIX ([Nagel](https://www.nber.org/papers/W17653)). The intraday studies did not make the news split, so "no-news flushes bounce more intraday" is a consistent extrapolation and not a measured intraday fact.

**Climax volume.** No rigorous intraday test was found showing that a volume climax marks the turn. Volume and volatility peak at the extreme and "stay significantly high long after", which makes "volume has peaked" hard to call in real time ([Zawadowski et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0406696)). At the daily level the relation between returns and volume "runs through order imbalances rather than through sheer trading volume" ([Chordia et al.](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~mkearns/finread/Chordia_buy-sell_orders.pdf)). The supported variable is the seller finishing, not the size of the volume bar.

**How his own numbers sit against this.** His data are more favourable than the old study and agree with it on shape. Turns reached VWAP 31 times in 95, and model turns 67% of the time; turns that snapped to VWAP within 30 minutes were best sold there; flush volume of 2 to 4 times normal won 66% (n=29) while more than 4 times won 41% (n=27); and moves of 1 to 1.5 ATR won 75% (n=20) against 39% beyond 1.5 ATR (n=23) ([Hold to VWAP study](https://app.notion.com/p/418821abbd8181e9ab6ff7b5b9adb397), [RVOL study](https://app.notion.com/p/418821abbd8181f3a591d2d696db240e)). Bigger and louder is not better in his sample, which fits "seller finished" over "volume climax". The documented average bounce of 15 to 20% of the drop is a reason to grade hard, since an average flush does not return to VWAP; his selected model turns are the exception that does. Twenty of his 33 turns that closed beyond VWAP stopped him first, which is what fading a seller who has not finished looks like.

## 10. Half-hour persistence is a next-day, same-clock, relative effect, and same-day it reverses

**Plain answer.** The result does not say an opening drive persists through the day. It says a stock that beat other stocks in a given half-hour tends to beat them again in the same half-hour on following days, for about 40 trading days. It holds in every half-hour, is several times stronger in the first and last, and is tiny. Within the same day the documented relation to the next half-hour is a reversal.

**What was measured.** The sample is 1,715 NYSE stocks from 2001 to 2005 across 13 half-hour intervals. The method is cross-sectional: it "subtracts an overall market effect ... and focuses on returns relative to other stocks" ([Heston, Korajczyk and Sadka](https://arxiv.org/abs/1005.3535)). Return responses are negative at the first several lags and then positive at exact multiples of 13 half-hours, meaning one day, two days, and so on. "Temporary price pressure is reversed at virtually all future times except at the same time interval on subsequent days."

**How big.** The top-minus-bottom decile spread earns **3.01 basis points** in the same half-hour the next day, and stays above 1 basis point for days two to five. By time of day the day-one spread is over 11 basis points in the opening half-hour, over 8 in the closing half-hour, and averages 1.75 from 10:00 to 15:30. After paying spreads the strategy returns about −24 to −25 basis points. The authors present it as an execution-timing saving, not a trading strategy.

**Same day.** Using quote-based returns to remove bid-ask bounce, "the only reversal that is statistically significant is that for k = 1", the immediately following half-hour, which the authors read as the market taking between 30 and 60 minutes to restore a liquidity shock. Beyond that lag there is essentially nothing.

**Worked examples.**

| Situation | What the result says | What it does not say |
|---|---|---|
| NVDA beats most stocks from 9:30 to 10:00 on Monday | Across many such stocks, the winners beat the losers by about 11 basis points from 9:30 to 10:00 on Tuesday. On a $100 stock that is 11 cents for the whole long-short spread. Averaged over all half-hours, the winners' side alone earns 1.66 basis points, under 2 cents | That NVDA trends up for the rest of Monday. That NVDA rises on Tuesday in absolute terms; the effect is relative to other stocks, so a falling market can take it down |
| The same Monday, 10:00 to 10:30 | The documented tilt is a small reversal of the 9:30 to 10:00 relative move | That the opening drive continues |
| A stock is relatively bid from 12:30 to 13:00 on Wednesday | A tilt of about 1.75 basis points in the same window on Thursday, significant statistically and invisible on a chart | Anything tradable for one stock on one day |
| A stock is relatively strong from 15:30 to 16:00 | A tilt of over 8 basis points in the last half-hour the next day; for S&P 500 names the closing half-hour dominates the whole effect | That it gaps up overnight; overnight returns were excluded |

**Does the day look the same?** No. Only the relative return in that window repeats, by a few basis points, across a portfolio of roughly 170 stocks a side. Price action, range and direction for one stock on one day are not predicted. Volume and volatility show the same daily rhythm, and adding them to the regression does not explain the return pattern. The authors' explanation, that institutions repeat the same execution schedule on successive days, is supported by a later study in which net orders from repetitive institutional traders absorb the predictability ([Murphy and Thirumalai](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfnres:v:40:y:2017:i:4:p:455-477)), though the authors themselves say no explanation fully accounts for it. No replication on post-2005 US data was found.

**The only same-day result is a different one.** For the S&P 500 ETF from 1993 to 2013, the return from the prior close to 10:00 predicts the last half-hour's return the same day, with an R-squared of about 1.6% ([Gao et al.](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:129:y:2018:i:2:p:394-414)). That is a market-level, weak effect and says nothing about single stocks. His practical reading should be: a real program that bought the first half-hour yesterday is slightly more likely to be buying the first half-hour today. It is a reason to revisit yesterday's one-sided names at the same clock time, and a reason to stop treating the opening drive as proof of a trend day.

## 11. Buybacks are a capped, patient, mostly invisible bid

**Plain answer.** Corporate buying is large in aggregate, about $4 billion a day for the S&P 500, heavily concentrated in about 20 names, and shaped by a safe-harbor rule that keeps it passive, away from the opening print and out of the last ten minutes. Nothing about it is disclosed in real time. The tape claims about a visible steady bid or support at VWAP have no evidence behind them.

### The rule that shapes the flow

Rule 10b-18 is a voluntary, day-by-day safe harbor from manipulation liability with four conditions; failing one loses the safe harbor for all that day's purchases, but trading outside it is not automatically unlawful ([SEC 10b-18 FAQ](https://www.sec.gov/divisions/marketreg/r10b18faq0504.htm)). **Documented**, at excerpt level:

| Condition | What it requires | Tape consequence (inference) |
|---|---|---|
| Manner | One broker or dealer per day | One desk, one schedule |
| Time | Not the opening regular-way purchase; nothing in the last 10 minutes for stocks with ADTV value of $1 million or more and float of $150 million or more; last 30 minutes for all others ([17 CFR 240.10b-18](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/240.10b-18)) | Absent from the open print, from 15:50 onward in liquid names, and from the closing auction |
| Price | Not above the higher of the highest independent bid and the last independent transaction price ([17 CFR 240.10b-18](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/240.10b-18)) | Never initiates an uptick to a new high; does not chase a breakout |
| Volume | No more than 25% of average daily volume over the four calendar weeks before the week of purchase; one block a week in lieu, with no other purchases that day ([govinfo CFR text](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2024-title17-vol4/pdf/CFR-2024-title17-vol4-sec240-10b-18.pdf), [Cravath](https://www.cravath.com/a/web/iyzUHjtbyLYCG9UtcuXW7L/auAstB/structuring-share-repurchases-rule-10b-18-and-rule-10b5-1.pdf)) | A ceiling far above typical use |

Compliance cannot be verified from public data, and in the only trade-level sample, from the 1990s, "only two firms in our sample consistently comply for all repurchases" ([Cook, Krigman and Leach](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ucp:jnlbus:v:76:y:2003:i:2:p:289-316)). The windows are strong tendencies, not absolutes.

### Size of the flow

S&P 500 buybacks were a record **$942.5 billion in 2024** and **$1.020 trillion in the 12 months to September 2025**, with quarterly figures of $293.5 billion, $234.6 billion and $249.0 billion for the first three quarters of 2025 ([S&P DJI Q4 2024](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/corporate-news/article/sp-500-q4-2024-buybacks-increase-74-and-2024-expenditure-sets-new-record-by-increasing-18-5), [S&P DJI Q3 2025](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/corporate-news/article/sp-500-q3-2025-buybacks-post-modest-62-gain-to-249-0-billion-after-declining-20-1-amidst-uncertainty-in-q2)). Official figures for Q4 2025 onward were not retrieved; a $1.10 trillion figure for the 12 months to June 2026 comes from a manager's note and is unverified ([Neuberger Berman](https://www.nb.com/insights/market-signals-equity-buybacks-shifting-buyer-base-drives-record-high-activity)). Reports on 2026 conflict, with Bloomberg describing record levels in May and Goldman, second-hand, describing growth that had stalled ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-05-04/-ultimate-dip-buyers-of-stocks-remain-strong-as-buybacks-surge), [Investing.com on Goldman](https://uk.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/sp-500-buyback-growth-has-stalled-after-record-firsthalf-pace--goldman-sachs-4256152)).

The flow is concentrated. In the 12 months to September 2025 Apple bought $96.7 billion, Alphabet $55.8 billion, NVIDIA $51.8 billion, Meta $44.2 billion and JPMorgan $27.6 billion, and the top 20 companies were 49.5% of the Q3 2025 total ([S&P DJI Q3 2025](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/corporate-news/article/sp-500-q3-2025-buybacks-post-modest-62-gain-to-249-0-billion-after-declining-20-1-amidst-uncertainty-in-q2)). At market level one estimate puts buybacks "close to 0.5% of daily equity trading volume" ([Elm Wealth](https://elmwealth.com/stock-buybacks/)). For the heaviest repurchasers the research notes' own arithmetic, using assumed average daily volumes, gives about 3 to 5% of a day's volume, far below the 25% cap; that is inference. The 1% excise tax on net repurchases "has not impacted overall buybacks" ([S&P DJI Q3 2025](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/corporate-news/article/sp-500-q3-2025-buybacks-post-modest-62-gain-to-249-0-billion-after-declining-20-1-amidst-uncertainty-in-q2)).

### Methods

Open-market repurchases dominate; a commonly quoted "almost 95%" is weakly sourced and no reliable split by method was found ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Share_repurchase)). The broker "often uses strategies similar to VWAP execution or schedule-based execution to spread the purchases through the day" ([Guzman practitioner's guide](https://www.guzman.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Share-Buybacks-for-US-Corporations-Practitioners-Guide-v-LinkedIn.pdf)), and one broker advises issuers to buy more aggressively when price is weak, flexing 10 to 20% around volume targets ([Raymond James](https://www.raymondjames.com/-/media/rj/dotcom/files/corporations-and-institutions/equity-capital-markets/ces_share_repurchase.pdf)). Both are **practice**. Preset 10b5-1 plans use formulas "based on the market price" that the issuer cannot touch once set ([Tech Data 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000790703/000119312511075076/d10k.htm)); they complete more and faster than plain programs ([Bonaimé, Harford and Moore](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2019.3322)), and the 2022 amendments imposed no cooling-off period on issuer plans ([Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2022/12/sec-amends-rules-for-rule-10b51-trading-plans-and-adds-new-disclosure-requirement)).

In an accelerated share repurchase the issuer pays a bank up front, receives most of the shares immediately from borrowed stock, and the bank buys in the market over a term of typically three to six months; the final share count is set by the average daily VWAP over the term less a discount ([Columbia Blue Sky](https://clsbluesky.law.columbia.edu/2026/08/12/accelerated-share-repurchases-corporate-governance-and-market-integrity/), [Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/-/media/files/perspectives-events/events/2018/07/west-legaledcenters-share-buybacks/files/presentation-materials/fileattachment/share-buybacks-7-24-18.pdf)). General Motors' November 2023 deal was $10 billion with about 16% of shares outstanding delivered up front. The buying on the tape during an ASR is the bank's hedge, and because the bank is short an average it has an incentive to buy more on days below the running average; that is a structural inference. An academic critique argues brokers with timing discretion can beat the "average of daily VWAPs" benchmark mechanically ([arXiv 2307.09617](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.09617)). Tender offers are announced, dated events and create no hidden intraday flow.

### Timing, blackouts and seasonality

An authorization is an option. Average completion is around half after one year, and 24% of US programs announced from 1985 to 2012 saw no buying in the fiscal year of announcement ([CFA Institute Research Foundation review](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/-/media/documents/book/rf-lit-review/2022/rflr-stock-buybacks.pdf)). Buying is "largely determined by the corporate calendar": the academic blackout runs from 20 days before fiscal quarter-end until three days after the next earnings announcement, and a blackout covering half a month cuts repurchase intensity by almost half the monthly average ([Dittmann, Li, Obernberger and Zheng](https://www.ecgi.global/sites/default/files/2025-06/equity-based-compensation-and-the-timing-of-share-repurchases.pdf)). Blackouts are company policy, not an SEC rule ([Marketplace](https://marketplace.org/2021/09/23/do-insider-trading-rules-apply-to-corporate-stock-buybacks)). Each company's own definition is filed as Exhibit 19 to its 10-K ([Sabre Exhibit 19](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1597033/000162828026008800/exhibit191sabrecorporation.htm)).

Flow falls in blackouts and does not stop, because preset plans keep running. A second-hand Goldman desk estimate says volumes "usually fall about 35%" ([The Wealth Advisor reprint of Bloomberg](https://www.thewealthadvisor.com/article/goldman-buyback-desk-was-flooded-orders-during-stock-rout)), which is **unverified**. The two published tests of whether the market is weaker in blackouts found no negative effect ([SSGA](https://www.ssga.com/library-content/pdfs/etf/us/b27-buyback-blackout-periods-do-not-negatively-impact-performance.pdf), [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/2019/01/buyback-blackout-periods-do-not-negatively-impact-market-performance/)). On seasonality two Goldman vintages disagree: a 2015 note had November busiest at about 13% of annual spending, and a 2025 note has November second at 10.1% behind August ([Ritholtz reprint of Bloomberg](https://ritholtz.com/2015/10/buyback-burst-seen-as-salvation-from-goldmans-sp-500-decline-2), [Tiger Brokers summary](https://www.itiger.com/news/1155391250)). Both fit post-earnings open windows. The per-stock inference is that an issuer's heaviest buying most plausibly falls in the five to seven weeks starting a few days after its own earnings date.

### What is disclosed, and when

The only mandatory data is the Item 703 table in each 10-Q and 10-K: for each month of the quarter, shares purchased, average price paid, shares bought under announced programs and remaining authorization ([Weil](https://www.weil.com/-/media/mailings/2024/q1/sec-issuer-repurchase-disclosures_-be-sure-to-look-at-the-correct-rules-02-26-24.pdf)). The SEC's 2023 rule that would have required daily repurchase data was vacated by the Fifth Circuit on 19 December 2023, along with the requirement to disclose issuer 10b5-1 plans ([SEC announcement](https://sec.gov/newsroom/whats-new/further-announcement-regarding-share-repurchase-disclosure-modernization-rule)). No replacement was found, and the notes treat "none as of November 2026" as probable but unconfirmed. He can know that an authorization exists and how much remains, whether an ASR is running, the company's blackout definition and earnings date, and last quarter's monthly pace. He cannot know whether the issuer is in the market today, the broker, the price grid, or the timing.

### Bargain buying, liquidity and support

Using the monthly price disclosures, firms repurchase below the average market price: about 2% less on average, and 6 to 8% less for infrequent repurchasers ([Dittmar and Field](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v115y2015i2p261-282.html)). The discount shrinks with firm size ([Ben-Rephael, Oded and Wohl](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/revfin/v18y2014i4p1299-1340..html)), so the large habitual repurchasers that dominate dollar flow get little of it, which implies steady, VWAP-like participation there. US studies find repurchases narrow spreads and improve liquidity ([Cook, Krigman and Leach](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v17y2004i2p463-498.html), [Hillert, Maug and Obernberger](https://pure.eur.nl/en/publications/stock-repurchases-and-liquidity/)); Hong Kong data find spreads widen on repurchase days ([Brockman and Chung](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/222513004_Managerial_timing_and_corporate_liquidity_evidence_from_actual_share_repurchases)). That is a real conflict, and the US reading is the relevant one for him. Repurchases reduce idiosyncratic risk, with effects "primarily driven by repurchases in down markets" ([Busch and Obernberger](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2016/11/16/actual-share-repurchases-price-efficiency-and-the-information-content-of-stock-prices/)). All of it is monthly or daily evidence. **No US study of the time of day of issuer purchases was found.**

### Announcement effects

Open-market announcements historically produced about a 3% short-window abnormal return, tender offers 8 to 15%, and accelerated repurchases about 1.2% ([Springer RQFA](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11156-010-0215-y), [Financial Analysts Journal](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.2469/faj.v66.n6.4)). Effects dropped after 2001 as repurchasers got larger. The classic 12.1% four-year drift from 1980 to 1990 data is disputed for recent samples ([Ikenberry, Lakonishok and Vermaelen](https://www.nber.org/papers/w4965.ris), [Peyer and Vermaelen](https://tevgeniou.github.io/EquityRiskFactors/bibliography/BuybacksPV.pdf)), and returns between authorization and completion are indistinguishable from zero ([Bargeron, Bonaimé and Thomas](https://cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/3A0AA39EF30BB41483794352CDB40BAC/S0022109017000084a.pdf/timing_and_source_of_longrun_returns_following_repurchases.pdf)). An announcement is a headline, often bundled with earnings, and not evidence that buying has started.

### What is and is not visible on the tape

| Claim | Rating |
|---|---|
| Issuer flow does not chase and never lifts to a new high | Documented (rule text) |
| Absent from the opening print, the last 10 minutes and the closing auction | Documented (rule; compliance voluntary and imperfect) |
| Spread through the day, VWAP-like | Practice |
| Heavier on weak days | Practice, plus documented at monthly frequency |
| Dampens volatility and supports price in down markets | Documented at monthly horizon only |
| Blackout removes the bid and the stock sags | Unverified; the two return tests find no effect |
| A steady visible bid, support at round numbers or at VWAP, an identifiable footprint | Unverified folklore; no source found |

A stock ramping into the close or gapping on the open print is not being moved by safe-harbor buyback flow. His IBM 7/16 "buy program" day, a relentless grind on 3.2 times relative volume with 2.57 million shares in the last five minutes and the close, cannot be attributed to a buyback from his data, and the closing volume in particular is outside the safe-harbor window ([IBM case study](https://app.notion.com/p/3a0821abbd8181a8a9c2e892053e7cb8)).

## 12. Unlocks and scheduled supply move volume permanently and price a little

**Plain answer.** The best-documented unlock effect is small: about −1.5% over three days with a permanent 40% rise in volume and no rebound, concentrated in venture-backed names. Calendar dates are often wrong because of early-release clauses that tie the unlock to earnings. The largest holders sell through bank-run blocks and distributions to fund investors, not by dribbling shares over the tape. In low floats the dominant supply event is the deeply discounted offering, which is anecdotal but consistent.

### Lock-up terms and why the calendar date is unreliable

The 180-day lock-up is a contract, not a rule ([StockAnalysis](https://stockanalysis.com/ipos/lockups/)). Larger recent deals carry staggered releases, price triggers and blackout pull-forwards that end the lock-up early when it would otherwise fall inside a trading blackout ([Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=994f3a80-3071-48e3-bdfb-5def2fd7108b), [Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2025/07/market-trends-2024-2025-lock-up-agreements)). CoreWeave's lock-up ended on 14 August 2025, about six weeks before the scheduled date, releasing 83% of Class A shares; Circle released 11.5 million shares early after the stock traded above a price trigger ([EquiLend](https://equilend.com/?p=51466)); Figma disclosed an early release of 25% of eligible employee securities with its first earnings report ([Sherwood News](https://sherwood.news/markets/figma-plunges-after-first-earnings-since-ipo-lock-up-expires-some-shareholders/)). The amount unlocking is often several times the float: only 9.5% of Arm had traded before its expiry ([Reuters via Yahoo](https://ca.finance.yahoo.com/news/arms-shares-rise-wall-street-194944362.html)), and about 82% of Reddit was subject to its date ([Barchart](https://www.barchart.com/story/news/27548628/why-reddit-stock-looks-too-risky-for-one-analyst-ahead-of-august-9)). These are **practice**-level case reports.

For a de-SPAC there are three supply dates: effectiveness of the resale registration for PIPE shares, which comes first and is on few calendars ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2025/06/SPAC-Re-Attack-Top-Considerations-for-Issuers-Considering-a-De-SPAC-Transaction)); the other holders' lock-up; and the sponsor lock-up, typically one year with early release if the stock trades above $12.00 for 20 of 30 trading days ([Harvard Law School Forum](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2018/07/06/special-purpose-acquisition-companies-an-introduction/)).

### Measured price effects

|---|---|---|

| No rebound in the following weeks; negative even with no reported insider sales ([Nowak review](https://folia.unifr.ch/documents/317901/files/nowak_AFE_2004.pdf)) | Same | Documented |

| No significant drop on the date itself but significant declines in the days before ([Emerald study](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/03074350410768859/full/html)) | US | Documented; conflicts with the first row on timing |
| Short selling rises before expiry and falls after; in venture- and private-equity-backed names it does not predict later returns ([Gibbs and Hao](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbfina/v88y2018icp30-43.html)) | Daily short data | Documented |

The sources disagree on whether the decline comes before or at the date. The reasonable reading is that price moves ahead of and into the date while the share flow starts on the date and persists. A −1.5% three-day effect sits well inside one day's range for a six-month-old IPO. Borrow gets expensive exactly when the short is wanted: new-borrow fees exceeded 70% for Peloton, which fell 17% on expiry day and left shorts roughly breaking even, while Tilray rose more than 24% into its expiry as shorts covered ([S&P Global](https://spglobal.com/marketintelligence/en/mi/research-analysis/borrow-costs-spike-ahead-of-preipo-lockup-expiries.html), [S&P Global](https://www.spglobal.com/marketintelligence/en/mi/research-analysis/short-selling-recent-ipos.html)).

Every violent drop had a second cause, either a named seller or earnings. With earnings-linked releases the unlock day and the first earnings reaction now often coincide, so the move cannot be cleanly attributed to supply.

### How holders sell, and the other scheduled supply

**Rule 144.** An affiliate's sales in any three months may not exceed the greater of 1% of the class outstanding or the average weekly volume over the preceding four weeks ([ABA Business Lawyer](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-lawyer/2026-winter/rule-144-opinions-report)). Form 144 is required above 5,000 shares or $50,000 and has been filed electronically on EDGAR since 2023, so notices are searchable the same day ([SEC guide](https://www.sec.gov/info/smallbus/secg/rules144-145-secg.htm)). For a hot recent IPO with heavy weekly volume the cap is not binding; it matters mainly in illiquid small caps (inference).

**10b5-1 insider plans.** Since 2023, directors and officers cannot trade under a new or modified plan until the later of 90 days after adoption or two business days after the next quarterly report, capped at 120 days; other persons wait 30 days ([SEC Release 33-11138](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/final/2022/33-11138.pdf)). Plan adoptions are disclosed in quarterly reports, which gives advance notice of programmatic insider selling. Plans that only sell shares to cover tax on vesting stock are exempt from the overlapping-plan limits ([NASPP](https://naspp.com/blog/Rule-10b5-1-Amendments)), and some issuers mandate automatic sell-to-cover ([DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/insights/publications/2025/03/tax-considerations-for-public-company-equity-incentive-awards)); that selling is price-insensitive and falls on vest dates.

**Venture distributions.** When a venture fund distributes shares to its investors, there is a substantial price reaction around the event and returns stay negative for months, consistent with funds timing distributions after run-ups ([Gompers and Lerner](https://econbiz.de/Record/venture-capital-distributions-short-run-and-long-run-reactions-gompers-paul/10005691887)). One holder becomes hundreds of uncoordinated sellers, which fits a months-long drift better than a one-day event.

**Execution style.** No source says whether unlocked holders use VWAP or TWAP algorithms or over how many days. That remains **unverified** lore. The evidence supports blocks for the largest holders and Form 144 and Form 4 visibility for affiliates.

**Secondaries and blocks.** Seasoned offerings were priced at an average discount of about 2 to 3% to the prior close in 1980s and 1990s samples, with a two-day announcement return near −3% ([Corwin](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v58y2003i5p2249-2279.html), [Mola and Loughran](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/discounting-and-clustering-in-seasoned-equity-offering-prices/CC91F820199DA02A6A341A832CAFF0AC)). From 2009 to 2014, 75% of offerings were announced and priced overnight; the 2.5% pre-issue decline that reverses within a week belongs to the older marketed format, and returns after overnight deals are less positive ([Gustafson](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v53y2018i02p837-866_00.html)). "Buy the secondary for the bounce" is therefore weaker for the format that now dominates. Reported 2025 deals priced 3.4% to 10% below the last close ([IFR on Primo](https://www.ifre.com/equities/2467102/primo-brands-slides-after-us489m-block), [IFR on MBX](https://www.ifre.com/equities/2318855/mbx-secures-us200m-from-marketed-follow-on)).

**At-the-market programs.** These are sold directly into the market and "dribbled out"; in 2016 their count was 63% and their proceeds 26% of those of seasoned offerings ([Billett, Floros and Garfinkel](https://core-cms.cambridgecore.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/atthemarket-offerings/BE5876A62A1E14B5C285F63D67C7FDE8)). Filed agreements let the issuer set a maximum number of shares per day and a minimum price below which the agent may not sell ([Gladstone Investment 8-K exhibit](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1321741/000119312524138399/d838923dex11.htm)). There is no announcement on selling days; confirmation arrives later in filings. A price floor means the supply tends to switch on into strength (inference).

**Convertibles.** Announcement returns averaged about −1.69% from 1984 to 1999 and about −4.59% from 2000 to 2008, a gap that disappears after controlling for arbitrage short selling ([University of Glasgow working paper](https://www.gla.ac.uk/media/media_199412_en.pdf)). Much of the day-of drop is mechanical hedging. A related study of 144A financings found an average 3.53% decline near announcement with a 2.35% recovery in the following month ([CFA Institute Digest](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/cfa-digest/2017/04/short-selling-pressure-and-last-resort-debt-finance-evidence-from-144a-high-yield-risk-adjust)).

**Low-float offerings.** The evidence is case reports, all pointing one way. Comstock priced 25.5% below the prior close ([Benzinga](https://benzinga.com/z/47084294)); Scorpius priced at $1 against a $5.63 close and fell 79% ([InvestorPlace](https://investorplace.com/?p=3156229)); Rubico fell 51.4% on a roughly $7.5 million deal with about 5.3 million shares outstanding ([Investing.com](https://ng.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/rubico-stock-plummets-after-pricing-dilutive-public-offering-93CH-2187845)). Against the 2 to 3% academic average these deals are an order of magnitude more dilutive. No systematic study of intraday behaviour around them was found, and the common view that price pins near the deal price because warrant holders can sell stock and keep the option is logic, not measurement.

**Index offset.** FTSE Russell excludes locked-up shares from free float and adds them back only at the next quarterly review after expiry ([FTSE Russell](https://www.lseg.com/content/dam/ftse-russell/en_us/documents/policy-documents/free-float-restrictions.pdf)). Passive demand from the float increase therefore arrives weeks to months after the unlock, at a rebalance close, and not on the day.

### Where to find the dates

A calendar site is the first pass, and one states plainly that its dates come from the prospectus and that early-release provisions can unlock shares sooner ([StockAnalysis](https://stockanalysis.com/ipos/lockups/)). The prospectus and later company filings are the authority; early releases are announced in earnings materials. Form 144 notices are on EDGAR the same day and Form 4s follow. At-the-market sales agreements are filed as Exhibit 1.1 to an 8-K ([Gladstone exhibit](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1321741/000119312524138399/d838923dex11.htm)). De-SPAC PIPE resale shelves are filed on Form S-1. The workflow the notes derive is: calendar, then read the prospectus lock-up language for earnings, price and blackout triggers and tranche sizes, then recompute once the first earnings date is set, then watch Form 144 on the morning of and after.

## 13. Actionable playbook

Every rule carries its rating. "Inference" means the rule follows from documented facts but has not itself been tested. His own data are from a simulated account with small samples, so "own data" is a reason to keep testing, not proof.

### Turn trades

| If | Then | Rating |
|---|---|---|
| The move is 1 ATR or more from VWAP in under 30 minutes | Treat it as urgent selling plus thin liquidity, not a schedule. Ask whether there is news | Inference from documented impact law |
| There is fundamental news behind the flush | Lower the grade. Informed flow is expected to retrace least; daily-horizon studies show news moves drift | Documented at daily horizon; intraday is extrapolation |
| There is no news and the name is in play (pace 1.2 or more) | This is his winning condition: counter-trend turns on relative volume of 1.2 or more won 59% at +0.49R (n=38) | Own data, moderate |

## Conclusion

The thread through all thirteen answers is that the flows he asked about are real and rule-bound, and almost none of them can be watched. Programs, buybacks, at-the-market sales and unlocked holders all trade patiently and mostly on the passive side, which is exactly the side his tools mislabel: delta counts a resting buyer as a seller, the round-lot filter removes the slices, and the session VWAP is not the benchmark anyone is paid against. What he can observe is state: how fast the stock is trading against its own curve, whether news sits behind the move, whether one-sided flow is still printing, and where the calendar puts earnings, blackouts and lock-ups. Each of those has at least some documented meaning, and his own records already rank most of them.

The corrections matter more than the additions. Two-thirds of an order's impact stays, so a finished buyer leaves a higher price and a finished seller a lower one; the turn trade works because waterfalls are urgency and thin liquidity, and those are a different animal from the scheduled orders the impact studies describe. That distinction is also the largest hole in the evidence. The only rebound table is two decades old, nothing tests climax volume, nothing measures unlock or buyback behaviour intraday, and nothing splits intraday reversals by news. His logged turns, tagged by news and measured at 10, 30 and 60 minutes, would be better evidence on his own question than anything the literature currently offers.
