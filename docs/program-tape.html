TWAP · VWAP · buy and sell programs · your turns, trend days and low floats

You can't see the program. You can see which state the stock is in.

Execution programs are real, slow and mostly passive. The research proves they leave traces, and nothing in it shows anyone can identify one on a public tape in real time, size it, or know when it ends. What is documented is how one-sided flow behaves while it lasts and after it stops. Your own records rank the same transitions.

What you can trade around

  • One-sided flow that lasts minutes to hours is mostly one participant splitting an order, and it persists. DOCUMENTED
  • When the order finishes, about a third of its impact comes back by the same close. DOCUMENTED
  • About 30% of parent orders run past one day and tend to reappear at the same half-hour. DOCUMENTED
  • Closing-auction moves revert overnight. Late continuous-session moves revert about five times less. DOCUMENTED

What the public tape cannot tell you

  • Who is trading, or what kind of participant it is.
  • The total size of the order, or how much is left.
  • Whether it is one parent order or several unrelated ones.
  • Whether the benchmark is VWAP, TWAP, percent of volume or arrival price.
  • When it ends.
  • Whether a held level is one order or many.

What that means for your read

Delta measures urgency, not accumulation. Signing rules get 76% to 81% of trades right and fail inside the quote. Negative delta with a price that will not fall is consistent with absorption. It is not proof. DOCUMENTED

A refreshing bid is a real mechanism. When a reserve order's shown size is used up, a new slice posts with a new timestamp. You see many prints at one price, more volume than was ever displayed, and size snapping back. DOCUMENTED

What you cannot tell apart: a reserve order, a fully hidden order, several traders re-posting, a market maker requoting, or an algorithm sending a new child order. It shows nothing about size left and can vanish on the next print. INFERENCE

Your rule already fits: the held bid is the entry, the dropped bid is the exit, a fast rebid is the re-entry. AMD 11/11 lost $204 when 530 broke four minutes in. OWN DATA

The indicator you can build

A pace gauge: today's volume against the stock's own curve

It measures conditions. It cannot show that a program has fallen behind, because the order's size, side and schedule are visible only to the broker. What it can say is what any scheduled order would be facing, if one exists.

Build it per symbol on 5-minute regular-hours bars over 20 sessions. Drop earnings, expiration and half days from the lookback, and keep the closing auction out. Ignore readings before about 10:00. Brokers mostly use this same simple historical curve, so your gauge approximates what the schedules were built from.

# Cumulative RVOL by time of day - UNTESTED DRAFT
# Intraday chart, extended hours OFF.
input days = 20;
def barsPerDay = RoundDown(23400000 / GetAggregationPeriod(), 0);
def newDay = GetYYYYMMDD() != GetYYYYMMDD()[1];
def cumVol = if newDay then volume else cumVol[1] + volume;
def sumPrior = fold i = 1 to days + 1 with s = 0

def avgPrior = sumPrior / days;
plot RVOLcum = if avgPrior > 0 then cumVol / avgPrior else Double.NaN;

Interval VWAP

Each order is graded against its own VWAP, not the line on your chart

The "interval" is the order's own life, from the moment the desk enters to the moment it finishes. It is not a clock interval. Move the start time to see the benchmark change.

Round times draw volume DOCUMENTED

Volume and trade count jumped about 20% at round 5, 10, 30 and 60-minute marks, with no change in trading costs. The sample is 2003 to 2006 and has no later replication.

Round times do not draw reversals UNVERIFIED

No study tests reversal frequency at 10:00 or 10:30. The one hard clock event is 15:50, when on-close entry ends and the imbalance is published. Scheduled economic releases are a plausible reason for turns near round times, so check the calendar.

After the order ends

Two-thirds of the push stays. One-third comes back.

Correction to "mean reverts 2/3 of the move"

It is the other way round. At that day's close, about two-thirds of a finished order's peak impact is still in the price (0.66 ± 0.04 across about 8 million orders). About one-third has reverted. A finished buyer leaves a higher price, and a finished seller a lower one.

The rise is the documented square-root shape. The decay points are arithmetic from the fitted curve in the study, in volume time: how much has traded since the order ended, relative to what traded while it ran. They are averages with very wide dispersion.

Why any of it comes back

While a big buyer works, sellers only supply stock at worse prices. Part of the rise is a fee for liquidity and fades when the buying stops. Part is information that someone large wants the stock, and that stays.

How fast

For an order that ran 30 minutes: about 10% back in the first few minutes, 20 to 30% after another 15 to 30 minutes of similar volume, and about a third once twice the order's volume has traded.

News changes it

Orders with no information behind them (cash flows) reverted almost completely in two to five days. Informed flow should retrace least. The tape does not say which you are watching, so news is your best proxy.

Your turn trade

The waterfall is not a VWAP or TWAP order

A scheduled order cannot move a stock 1 ATR in 20 to 30 minutes. That takes news, urgent selling at very high participation, several sellers at once, or liquidity providers stepping back, usually together.

Price push in a 20-minute window, in daily volatility INFERENCE

Square-root law arithmetic. An all-day VWAP order for 10% of the day's volume pushes price about a third of a day's volatility over the whole session. Stop cascades and forced liquidation are the usual desk explanations for the rest and are unmeasured.

The only measured bounce after extreme drops DOCUMENTED

Liquid US stocks, 2000 to 2002, drops of 4% or more inside an hour. The bounce was 15 to 20% of the drop, mostly in the first 10 to 30 minutes, with nothing further from 60 to 120 minutes. The sample is old and more extreme than a 1 ATR flush.

What to expect from a turn

The average flush does not return to VWAP. Your graded model turns are the exception: 67% reached it. That is the case for grading hard.

News behind the flush lowers the grade. At daily horizons, moves with news drift and moves without news reverse. Nobody has split intraday bounces by news. DOCUMENTED

The half-hour effect

Same clock window, next day. Not the rest of today.

A stock that beat other stocks in a half-hour tends to beat them again in the same half-hour on following days, for about 40 sessions. It does not say an opening drive persists. Within the same day the next half-hour slightly reverses.

      Buybacks

      A capped, patient bid you cannot see

      Corporate buying is large and concentrated, and a safe-harbor rule keeps it passive. Nothing about it is disclosed in real time: purchases are reported as monthly totals, weeks after the quarter ends.

      When a safe-harbor purchase is allowed DOCUMENTED

      body of the day
      9:30 not the opening print15:50 → close: none (liquid names)
      • Price: never above the highest independent bid or the last independent trade. It cannot lift to a new high or chase a breakout.
      • Volume: at most 25% of four-week average daily volume. Heavy repurchasers run at an estimated 3 to 5%.
      • One broker per day. Less liquid names stop 30 minutes before the close.

      The safe harbor is voluntary and compliance is imperfect, so treat the windows as strong tendencies. Check the rule wording against eCFR before relying on it.

      Finding the real date

      • Start from a lock-up calendar, then read the prospectus for earnings, price and blackout triggers and tranche sizes.
      • Recompute once the first earnings date is set.
      • Watch Form 144 on EDGAR the morning of and after. It is filed the same day for affiliate sales.
      • On low floats, check for an effective shelf or a live at-the-market agreement (Exhibit 1.1 to an 8-K) before any long.
      Indicators to build

      Ten gauges, each tied to something measured

      None of these detects a program. Each one measures a state that the research or your own records give a meaning to. The first four cover most of the value.

      1. Pace gaugeOWN DATA

      Computes: today's cumulative volume ÷ the 20-day average at the same time.

      Reads: under 1.2 not in play, 1.2 to 2 normal, 2+ news-day volume, falling after a hot open means urgent flow is finishing.

      Your turns split on exactly these lines. Draft script is above.

      2. Flush meterOWN DATA

      Computes: distance from VWAP in ATR, minutes the run has lasted, and run volume as a multiple of normal.

      Reads: green at 1 to 1.5 ATR with 2 to 4× volume. Amber beyond 1.5 ATR or over 4×.

      Bigger and louder won less in your sample (75% against 39%, 66% against 41%).

      3. Seller-still-working flagOWN DATA

      Computes: how many bars of the run touched or closed back to the 2-minute 9 EMA, and whether pops are being sold.

      Reads: a run riding the 9 EMA is a grind. No turn yet.

      +0.24R for clean runs against −0.29R when riding. Split orders persist for hours.

      4. Room-to-VWAP labelOWN DATA

      Computes: (VWAP − entry) ÷ (entry − stop), live on the chart.

      Reads: under 2R is C size or a log entry.

      INTC 10/30 and AMD 11/11 were both under 1R to VWAP.

      5. Bounce clockDOCUMENTED

      Computes: minutes since the low and the share of the flush retraced, with marks at 10, 30 and 60 minutes.

      Reads: the measured bounce was mostly done by 30 to 60 minutes. Past 60 with no progress, stop waiting.

      It also logs the bounce table the research is missing.

      6. One-sided day counterOWN DATA

      Computes: share of bars closed on one side of VWAP since the reclaim, distance above VWAP, and position in the day's range.

      Reads: distance and range position at 1:00 PM predicted a close at the high (0.72 and 0.73). The pullback-and-hold did not (0.51).

      It confirms buying already happened. It does not say VWAP will hold.

      7. Event-anchored VWAPsINFERENCE

      Computes: VWAP anchored at the flush low, the reclaim, and the news time, next to the session line.

      Reads: shows where flow that started at that event is averaged. Desks are graded on interval VWAP, not the session line.

      A reference, not a level anyone is shown to defend.

      8. Give-back trackerDOCUMENTED

      Computes: after a one-way run, the share of the push retraced and the volume traded since the run stopped.

      Reads: the average is about 10% back quickly and about a third by the close. A fade well past that says the flow was not informed.

      Use it as an exit cue on trend-day cores.

      9. Low-float state lightOWN DATA

      Computes: standard deviations above VWAP and percent of the last leg retraced.

      Reads: over 1 SD above with under 37% retraced: new high in 30 minutes about 60% of the time. Below VWAP: 7 to 8%.

      Use it to hold or size up a winner. Every mechanical entry on it lost.

      10. Calendar and clock flagsDOCUMENTED

      Computes: chart labels for buyback open window or blackout, lock-up date, a line at 15:50, and shading on the half-hour windows that were one-sided yesterday.

      Reads: context only. After 15:50 issuer buying is gone and auction moves revert overnight.

      Cheap to build and stops you assigning flow to the wrong cause.
